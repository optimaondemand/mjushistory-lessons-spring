colonization (kol-uh-nih-ZAY-shun): In this lesson, a plan to send free Black Americans to settle in Africa.

emancipation (ih-man-sih-PAY-shun): Setting slaves free.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did the men who wrote “all men are created equal” not end slavery?

The Declaration of Independence says that all men are created equal. Thomas Jefferson wrote those words. Yet Jefferson owned hundreds of slaves over his lifetime, and so did James Madison. This lesson asks how the founders thought about slavery, what they did about it, and why their children and grandchildren inherited the problem.

An Old Institution

Slavery was in America almost from the beginning. The first Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619. By the time of the Revolution, about half a million slaves lived in the thirteen colonies, in the North as well as the South. It was not new in 1776, and it was legal almost everywhere in the world.

Concessions for Independence

At independence, the founders made hard bargains. Jefferson’s first draft of the Declaration blamed King George for forcing the slave trade on the colonies. Delegates from South Carolina and Georgia objected, and Congress took out the passage. The Constitution made more concessions. It set a rule for counting each state’s population to assign seats in the House: all free persons plus three-fifths of the slave population. This was a compromise between Southern delegates, who wanted all counted, and Northern delegates, who wanted none counted. It required that people who escaped slavery be returned. It allowed the slave trade to continue until 1808.

Some progress was made. In 1787 the Northwest Ordinance banned slavery north of the Ohio River. Northern states began to end slavery one by one. Pennsylvania passed a gradual emancipation law in 1780, in which children born to slave mothers would become free at a set age. Others followed. But in the South, where most slaves lived, slavery stayed.

Slaves had their own ideas about freedom. In 1775 the British governor of Virginia promised freedom to slaves who left their masters and joined the British. Thousands tried to escape. In 1777 a group of Black slaves in Massachusetts sent a petition to the legislature. They used the language of the Revolution to ask for their own freedom. Those who fought for liberty were not all in the Continental Army.

Jefferson’s Troubles

In 1785 Jefferson published Notes on the State of Virginia. In Query XVIII, he wrote that slavery corrupted both the slave and the master. He wrote, “I tremble for my country when I reflect that God is just.” He feared that slavery would bring the nation’s punishment. But in the same book Jefferson also wrote racial claims about Black Americans that were unproven and untrue. Those claims helped later generations excuse slavery.

In 1791 Jefferson received a letter from Benjamin Banneker, a free Black farmer and astronomer from Maryland. Banneker sent him an almanac he had made and asked how a man who believed in liberty could still hold others in bondage. Jefferson replied politely and did little.

Jefferson did not free most of the people he held. He freed only a handful of them. In 1820 he would describe the problem this way: “we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go.” In other words, slavery was wrong, but he could not see a safe way to end it.

The Colonization Idea

Many Southern leaders, including Madison and James Monroe, could not imagine a country where Black and white people lived together as equals. They turned to colonization. In 1816 they helped found the American Colonization Society, which planned to send free Black Americans to Africa. In 1822 the Society founded the colony of Liberia in West Africa.

Most free Black Americans rejected the idea. They were born here, and this was their country. In 1817 about three thousand Black men met in Philadelphia and condemned the plan. And colonization was tiny. It sent only a few thousand people, and the slave population kept growing.

Leaving It to Later

Why did so many leaders expect slavery to fade away? Part of the reason was money. Tobacco was wearing out Virginia’s soil, and many planters were losing money. Another part was the law. Congress could end the slave trade in 1808, and many expected that to weaken slavery. George Washington, who held hundreds of people, freed those he owned in his will. Few large slaveholders did the same. Hopes like these were common among the founders, and they turned out to be wrong.

Jefferson and Madison hoped that slavery would fade out, and that future generations would find a way to make the country consistently free without a disaster. Some Americans, mostly Northerners, expected at least gradual emancipation. But Jefferson’s generation did not act, and what they left was a bigger problem than the one they inherited. 💡 A Question to Weigh
A republic is supposed to bind all its people into one community with a common good. What does it say about a republic when a large group of its people are held in bondage? Jefferson himself saw the problem. Historians still debate whether another path was possible.
