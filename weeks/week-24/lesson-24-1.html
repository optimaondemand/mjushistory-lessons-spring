📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

civil disobedience (SIV-ul dis-uh-BEE-dee-ens): Refusing to obey a law you believe is unjust, and accepting the punishment openly.

Transcendentalism (tran-sen-DEN-tul-iz-um): A movement of writers who taught that each person should trust his own conscience and learn from nature.

utopian community (yoo-TOH-pee-un kuh-MYOO-nih-tee): A group that tries to build a perfect society by living and working together under shared rules.

📖 Today's Focus
What did Americans mean by “progress,” and who was included?

After the Awakening, many Northerners believed that a good society could be built if enough people tried. But they disagreed about how. Some said to trust yourself. Some said to build better schools and hospitals. Some said to start over in a new community. And some said that the nation had not yet kept the promise of the Declaration of Independence. This lesson follows four answers.

Trust Yourself

Ralph Waldo Emerson was a former minister who became one of the most famous writers in America. He led a group of writers who called their ideas Transcendentalism. In “Self-Reliance” (1841), Emerson told readers to trust their own conscience and not to follow the crowd. The Transcendentalists also loved nature, and thought it taught truths that books and churches could not.

Emerson’s friend Henry David Thoreau took the idea further. In 1845 he built a small cabin at Walden Pond near Concord, Massachusetts, and lived there for two years. His book Walden (1854) is about living simply. Earlier, in 1846, Thoreau had been jailed for one night for refusing to pay a poll tax. He did not want to support a government that allowed slavery and was fighting the Mexican War. His essay on the experience, now called “Civil Disobedience,” argued that a person should break an unjust law openly and accept the punishment. This idea is called civil disobedience. It would later inspire Martin Luther King Jr.

Other great writers of the period, such as Nathaniel Hawthorne and Herman Melville, took a darker view. Hawthorne wrote about guilt and hidden sin in The Scarlet Letter (1850). Melville wrote Moby-Dick (1851) about a captain whose pride destroys his ship. They warned that human beings are not as perfect as the optimists believed.

Better Schools, Better Care

Horace Mann believed that a republic needs educated citizens. As head of the Massachusetts Board of Education starting in 1837, he pushed for common schools, which were free public schools open to all children, and for schools to train teachers. Mann called free public schooling “the great equalizer of the conditions of men.” He believed that children of rich and poor families who learned side by side would grow up to be better citizens. Many Northern states followed his lead. The South, with its scattered farms and plantations, built far fewer public schools.

Dorothea Dix, a schoolteacher, visited a Massachusetts jail in 1841 and found mentally ill people locked in cold, filthy cells. She spent the next years visiting jails and poorhouses across the country and reporting what she saw. Her reports helped convince states to build hospitals to care for people with mental illness. Temperance societies pushed in a similar way. Many reformers believed that drunkenness broke up families, and they wanted laws against selling alcohol.

Starting Over

Some reformers gave up on fixing the old society and tried to start a new one. They built utopian communities. At Brook Farm in Massachusetts (1841), writers and thinkers shared work and farming. Hawthorne lived there for a while. At Oneida, New York, a preacher named John Humphrey Noyes started a community with its own unusual rules about marriage and property. The Shakers, who lived in celibate villages, were known for their simple, fine furniture.

Did these experiments work? Not for long. Brook Farm lost money and closed in 1847 after a fire. Most of the communities fell apart because sharing everything is hard when people have different goals and work habits. A community that cannot produce enough to feed itself cannot stay together, no matter how good its ideas are. You have seen this before. The colonists at Jamestown and Plymouth tried sharing their food, and it nearly ruined them. The Shakers lasted longest, but their numbers shrank because they did not have children.

Seneca Falls

In 1840 Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton went to an antislavery convention in London. The men in charge would not let women speak. Mott and Stanton were angry. Eight years later they organized a meeting in Seneca Falls, New York, in July 1848. About 300 people came.

Stanton wrote a Declaration of Sentiments, copying the language of the Declaration of Independence. It declared that “all men and women are created equal.” It listed the wrongs done to women: they could not vote, in most states married women could not own property, and they were shut out of most jobs and colleges. The hardest demand to win was the vote. Even Mott hesitated. Frederick Douglass, the former slave and newspaper editor, spoke for it, and it passed by a narrow margin. About a hundred people signed. It would take 72 years for women to win the vote nationwide. Notice that many of the women at Seneca Falls were also abolitionists. They had learned to speak and organize in the antislavery movement, and they saw the two causes as connected.

Whose Progress?

Reform multiplied in the North, but the South did not welcome it. Many Southerners saw reform as an attack on their way of life. And the biggest reform of all, ending slavery, still had no political answer. The next lesson turns to the men who left this problem to the next generation.
