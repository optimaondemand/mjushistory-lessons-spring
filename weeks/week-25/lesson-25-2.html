abolitionist (ab-uh-LISH-un-ist): A person who worked to end slavery.

gag rule (GAG ROOL): A rule that blocks a subject from being discussed. Here, it kept Congress from hearing antislavery petitions.

petition (puh-TISH-un): A written request, signed by many people, asking the government to act.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did Southern leaders stop calling slavery an evil and start calling it good?

Thomas Jefferson called slavery a wrong that no one knew how to end. By the 1830s many Southern leaders no longer said that. They said slavery was right, and that it should last forever. This lesson explains what changed. It also shows why a quarrel that once seemed far away now stood in the middle of national politics.

The Argument Changes

Several events shook the South in the early 1830s. In January 1831 William Lloyd Garrison, a Boston editor, began printing a newspaper called The Liberator. He demanded the immediate end of slavery, and he refused to compromise. In 1833 the British Empire ended slavery in its colonies. Northern abolitionists began mailing antislavery pamphlets into the South.

Then, in August 1831, Nat Turner, a slave preacher in Southampton County, Virginia, led a revolt. He and his followers killed about sixty white men, women, and children. Militia and mobs struck back and killed many Black people, most of whom had nothing to do with the revolt. Turner was captured and hanged in November. White Southerners were terrified.

Virginia’s legislature debated ending slavery in the winter of 1831 and 1832. Some members spoke openly against it. In the end, they voted to do nothing. After that, the lawmakers of many Southern states passed harsher laws. It became a crime in many places to teach slaves to read, and public meetings of Black people were restricted. A door that had been slightly open was shut. Southerners began to call slavery their “peculiar institution.” Peculiar meant particular to them, special to the South. The phrase was meant to sound proud. Many Northerners, and many people in other countries, heard something else in it.

Calhoun’s Positive Good

John C. Calhoun of South Carolina was one of the sharpest minds in the Senate. He had been Vice President and had once been a nationalist. By the 1830s he had become the leading voice of the South. In a Senate speech on February 6, 1837, he rejected what he called the old idea that slavery was an evil. He said instead that it was “a positive good.”

He gave several arguments. First, he said that slavery appeared in the Bible. Second, he said that every great civilization, from Athens to Rome, had been built on the work of a laboring class held in bondage. Third, he argued that slaves in the South were better cared for than factory workers in the North. And he claimed that Black Americans were not equal to white Americans and needed a master. Many Southern ministers repeated these arguments from the pulpit, and Southern colleges taught them. Pro-slavery writers also said that slavery protected workers from the cruelty of wage labor, which is a claim you can test against what you learned earlier.

💡 Fact or Opinion?
Historians separate facts from opinions. It is a fact that Calhoun made this speech and that Southerners adopted its arguments. It is an opinion that slaves were better off. The evidence says otherwise: families were sold apart, people were whipped, and many risked their lives to escape. When you read a source, ask which statements can be checked and which cannot.

The Gag Rule

Abolitionists also used a legal tool that the Constitution protects: the petition. The First Amendment protects the right to ask the government to fix a problem. By the mid-1830s, antislavery citizens were sending thousands of petitions to Congress. Southern members were furious.

In 1836 the House passed a gag rule. It automatically tabled antislavery petitions without reading them. John Quincy Adams, who had left the presidency and returned to the House, fought the rule for eight years. He said it violated the right of petition. In 1844 the House finally repealed it. The fight made many Northerners ask whether the South’s defense of slavery was beginning to threaten their own freedoms.

A Society Built on Hierarchy

Calhoun’s vision of the South was a society with a clear order. In his picture, the planter class led, and slave workers served. He thought that equality was a dangerous idea. Many Southerners agreed. They praised tradition, family, and honor. Some of those habits, such as loyalty and hospitality, were admirable in themselves. But a society built on the permanent bondage of millions of people could not bind them into the same community as everyone else, and it could not claim the Declaration’s promise for them.

Calhoun’s own life shows how much one man’s choices could matter. His intelligence and force gave Southerners a way of defending themselves. If he had argued for a gradual end to slavery, as Jefferson at least imagined, the South might have followed another path. He chose otherwise, and the South followed him.

Compromise Gets Harder

Until now, Northerners and Southerners had compromised by treating slavery as a tolerated evil that would eventually fade. If Southerners now said that slavery was a positive good, compromise meant protecting something they called good. Northerners would say that a permanent slave society could not be reconciled with the Declaration’s claim that all men are created equal. The next lessons show how the nation tried to compromise anyway.
