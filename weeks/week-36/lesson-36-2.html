📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

Enforcement Acts (en-FORS-ment akts): Laws passed by Congress in 1870 and 1871 that made it a federal crime to use force to keep citizens from voting.

Redeemers (rih-DEE-merz): White Southern Democrats who took back control of the Southern state governments in the 1870s. They said they were “redeeming” the South.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did the nation stop protecting the rights it had just written into the Constitution?

Freedom had gains, but it also had enemies. Beginning in the late 1860s, groups of white Southerners used violence to take back power. At the same time, Northern voters grew tired of the struggle. This lesson follows the end of Reconstruction, and then looks back at the whole course.

Violence

In 1866, a group of former Confederate soldiers in Pulaski, Tennessee, formed a club called the Ku Klux Klan. It soon became a secret organization that used terror against Republicans, both Black and white. Members wore robes and masks, rode at night, and burned homes, churches, and schools. They whipped and murdered people to keep them from voting. Many Black Southerners and white Republicans kept voting and serving despite the threats. Similar groups, such as the White League in Louisiana and the Red Shirts in South Carolina, appeared in the 1870s.

One of the worst attacks was at Colfax, Louisiana, on Easter Sunday in 1873. After a disputed election, armed Black men and others gathered at the courthouse to protect it. A white force attacked and killed at least sixty of them, and probably more, many after they had surrendered.

The Government Responds

President Grant took the problem seriously. Congress passed the Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871. They made it a federal crime to use force to stop people from voting. Grant created the Department of Justice and sent federal prosecutors and soldiers to the South. In 1871 he used the new law to send troops into nine counties of South Carolina, and he arrested hundreds of Klan members. Congress held hearings where victims told their stories, and you will read some of that testimony. The Klan was largely broken up for a time.

But the Supreme Court weakened the law. In a case called Cruikshank in 1876, the Court ruled that the Fourteenth Amendment limited what states could do, so the federal government could not use it to punish private individuals for attacking citizens. The decision made it harder to prosecute those who used terror.

Northern Fatigue

Meanwhile, the North was losing interest. In 1873 a financial crash called the Panic of 1873 began a long depression. Northerners focused on jobs and money instead of the South. Scandals in Grant’s administration hurt the Republicans. Many Northerners felt that the South should solve its own problems. In 1874 the Democrats won control of the House of Representatives. Without Northern support, the Southern Republican governments stood alone.

Redeemers

In the South, white Democrats called Redeemers took back state after state, using violence, fraud, and pressure on voters. Some fired workers or refused credit to Black Republican voters. By 1876 only three Southern states, South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida, still had Republican governments.

The Election of 1876

The presidential election that year was very close. The Democrat, Samuel Tilden, won more popular votes than the Republican, Rutherford B. Hayes. But the electoral votes of South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida were disputed, and so was one vote from Oregon. Whoever won them would win the presidency. A special commission of fifteen members, with a Republican majority, gave all the disputed votes to Hayes. He won by one electoral vote, 185 to 184.

Southern Democrats threatened to block the result. A series of private deals followed. Many historians believe that Democrats agreed to accept Hayes if he withdrew the last federal troops from the South. Hayes did so in April 1877. This is called the Compromise of 1877, and it marks the end of Reconstruction. In Florida, the Democrat George Drew took office as governor in January 1877.

What the War Settled

Look back at the whole course. In 1788 the Constitution joined thirteen states. George Washington warned that parties built on sections of the country would one day threaten the Union. Compromise after compromise kept the peace until 1860, and then it failed. Lincoln had said in 1858 that the house could not stand half slave and half free. In the end, it became all one thing. The war cost at least 620,000 lives.

Remember the question that ran through this course: who decides? Marshall said the Court. Calhoun said the states. The war answered one part of it, because states could not leave the Union. But in the 1870s the Court, the voters, and Congress decided how far the new amendments would reach, and they chose not to press hard.

The war settled that no state could leave the Union. It ended slavery, and it added the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments to the Constitution. But it left questions unsettled. After 1877 the amendments remained in the Constitution but were poorly enforced. In the years that followed, Southern states passed laws known as Jim Crow laws that separated the races and kept many Black citizens from voting. Equal citizenship would wait for a later generation. This is the question for your final discussion, and for the Quarter 4 Capstone.
