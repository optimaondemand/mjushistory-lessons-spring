Lesson 7.3
Freedpeople Build New Lives Under Federal Protection
Lesson · ~850 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

crop-lien system (KROP-LEEN SIS-tum): A system in which a store lent a farmer goods on credit, and the farmer’s next crop was held as payment.

sharecropping (SHAIR-krop-ing): A system in which a farmer works land owned by someone else and pays for it with a share of the crop.

📖 Today's Focus
What did freedpeople build in the years after slavery, and what held them back?

Slavery was over. Now four million people had to build lives for themselves, with little money, no land, and a government that was still fighting over what to do. This lesson looks at what they built in the first years of freedom, and at what limited them.

Families and Churches

The first thing many freedpeople did was look for family. Husbands, wives, and children had been separated by sale. After the war, people traveled across the South to find one another. Newspapers printed notices that began, “Information wanted.” Many couples who had lived together as husband and wife for years now married in a legal ceremony.

Freedpeople also built their own churches. Before the war, many had been forced to worship under white supervision. Now they started their own congregations, mostly Baptist and Methodist, including the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church. The church became a meeting place, a school, and a center of political life.

A Hunger for Schools

Many freedpeople wanted to read, because it was a way to protect themselves, to read the Bible, and to sign contracts. They set up schools in churches and cabins and paid for teachers themselves. The Freedmen’s Bureau helped, along with Northern aid groups. Teachers came from the North, and many were Black. New colleges opened in these years, including Fisk University in Nashville and Howard University in Washington, D.C. Both are still open.

The Question of Land

Most freedpeople had worked the land all their lives, and many believed they were owed a piece of it. In January 1865 General Sherman had set aside land on the coast of South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida to be divided into forty-acre farms. Rumors spread of “forty acres and a mule.” But in 1865 President Johnson returned most of that land to its pardoned owners. Thaddeus Stevens asked Congress to take land from large planters and give it to the freed families, but Congress would not. Many Republicans believed strongly in the protection of property, and they did not want to take land from anyone.

So freedpeople had freedom but almost no land and little money. Planters had land but few workers they could pay in cash. Both sides had to find a way to work together.

Sharecropping

The answer that emerged was sharecropping. A freedperson’s family would rent a plot from a landowner. In return, the landowner took a share of the crop, often half. Freedpeople liked it better than gang labor under an overseer, because each family worked its own plot and made its own decisions. But there was a trap. Sharecroppers needed seed, tools, and food before the crop came in, so they bought them on credit at a local store. This was the crop-lien system. If the crop failed or prices fell, the family could not pay the debt. Many ended each year owing more than they had earned. The cycle repeated, and it was hard to escape.

Voting and Office

Under the Reconstruction Acts, Black men voted for the first time in 1867 and 1868. Many joined the Republican Party. They joined local clubs called Union Leagues, which taught members about voting, and they elected Black officials in large numbers. In Mississippi, Hiram Revels, a minister and teacher, became the first Black member of the U.S. Senate in 1870. He took the seat that Jefferson Davis had once held. Blanche K. Bruce, also of Mississippi, was elected to the Senate in 1874 and served a full term. About sixteen Black men served in Congress during Reconstruction, and hundreds more served in state legislatures.

Florida had its own leaders. Jonathan Gibbs, a Dartmouth graduate and minister, served as Florida’s Secretary of State and later as its Superintendent of Public Instruction. Josiah Walls, a former soldier in the Union army, was elected to Congress from Florida in 1870. They worked under the 1868 constitution you studied in Lesson 7.2.

Southern Republican Governments

The Republican governments in the South were built on a coalition. It included freedpeople, white Southerners who supported the Union, and Northerners who had moved south. Their opponents gave these groups insulting names, such as “scalawag” and “carpetbagger.” These governments built the first public school systems in many Southern states, repaired roads and bridges, and built railroads, hospitals, and asylums. They also raised taxes to pay for all this. Critics charged them with waste and corruption. Some of it was real. But corruption was common in those years across the whole country, North and South.

Gains and Dangers

In just a few years, freedpeople made real gains in education, family life, and political rights. But they were still dependent. Without land, most had to work for others. Their rights depended on federal protection and on a political coalition in the South. Both were fragile. In the next lesson you will see what happened when they began to fail. You will also read a letter from Jourdon Anderson, a freedman who moved to Ohio and answered his former master’s offer to come back.
