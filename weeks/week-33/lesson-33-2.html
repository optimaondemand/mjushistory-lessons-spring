Lesson 33.2
The War Reaches Every Home
Lesson · ~820 words · ~7 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

conscription (kun-SKRIP-shun): A law that forces people to serve in the army. It is also called the draft.

homestead (HOME-sted): A farm and home. Under the Homestead Act, a settler could claim 160 acres of public land.

inflation (in-FLAY-shun): A rise in prices that makes money buy less than before.

📖 Today's Focus
What did the Civil War ask of people who never fired a shot?

The war was not only fought by armies. By 1863 it touched almost every farm, shop, and kitchen in the country. Both governments had to find soldiers, raise money, and keep order. In this lesson you will see what that cost the people at home, and how the two sides held up.

Calling Men to the Army

At first both armies were filled by volunteers. But as the casualty lists grew, fewer men signed up. In April 1862 the Confederacy passed the first national draft in American history. Men from 18 to 35 had to serve. This law of conscription was a surprise from a government built to defend the rights of states. In March 1863 the Union followed. But the Union draft allowed a man to avoid service by paying $300 or by hiring a substitute. Many working men said it was a rich man’s war and a poor man’s fight.

In July 1863, just after Gettysburg, a mob in New York City rioted against the draft for four days. The rioters burned buildings, attacked Black residents, and killed more than a hundred people. Soldiers fresh from Gettysburg had to be sent to restore order.

Shortages in the South

The Union blockade hurt the South. Goods grew scarce, and the Confederate government printed paper money to pay its bills. This caused inflation, so a loaf of bread cost more each month. In April 1863, a crowd of women in Richmond, hungry and angry, broke into stores shouting for bread. President Davis begged them to go home and threatened to have soldiers fire on them. Desertion grew too. Soldiers received letters from wives saying that children were hungry, and some went home to help.

Liberty in Wartime

A war also tests how far a government may go. In 1861 Lincoln suspended the right to challenge your arrest in court (habeas corpus) in some places, so that suspected rebels could be held without a trial. Chief Justice Taney said only Congress had that power. Lincoln went ahead, and Congress approved the practice in 1863. Thousands of people were arrested. One Ohio politician, Clement Vallandigham, was jailed for criticizing the war and sent behind Confederate lines. Lincoln argued that if a government cannot defend itself, it cannot protect anyone’s rights. Critics called him a tyrant. The question of how far the government can go in wartime was left open.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

habeas corpus (HAY-bee-us KOR-pus): A Latin phrase for the right to ask a court to decide whether your arrest is lawful.

A Republican Congress Builds

With Southern members gone, the Republicans in Congress passed a series of laws that would shape the nation. The Homestead Act of 1862 gave 160 acres of western land to any settler who farmed it for five years. The Morrill Act gave states land to sell to pay for colleges, which became state universities. The Pacific Railway Act sent a railroad to the Pacific, to be built by two companies. Congress also created a national paper currency, called greenbacks. The North’s farms and factories grew during the war, and the South’s economy fell apart.

A homestead was the kind of dream the Free Soil movement had talked about, a family farm worked by free hands on free land. Before the war, Southern senators had blocked such a law.

Women at Work

Women on both sides ran farms, managed shops, and took jobs in government offices and factories. Many served as nurses. Clara Barton brought supplies to the wounded and nursed soldiers at the front, including at Antietam. The U.S. Sanitary Commission, a private group, raised money and sent supplies. About 20,000 women worked in Union hospitals. In the South, women sewed uniforms, ran plantations, and cared for the wounded in homes and churches.

Mary Chesnut, the wife of a Confederate officer, kept a diary of the war in South Carolina and Richmond. She wrote about the hardships, the hopes, and the fears of the people around her. You will read a passage from it.

Florida on the Home Front

In Florida, thousands of men left for the armies in Virginia and Tennessee, and families at home struggled to farm and find food. In February 1864, a Union force marched inland from Jacksonville to cut the railroad and win control of part of the state. At Olustee, near Lake City, they met a Confederate army. It was the largest battle fought in Florida. The Confederates won, and the Union lost about 1,800 men. Several of the Union regiments were Black troops, including the 54th Massachusetts. Florida’s cattle and salt kept feeding Confederate armies, and Tallahassee remained the only Southern capital east of the Mississippi that Union troops never took.

A Question Left Open

By 1864 the North’s economy was booming and the South’s was collapsing. But the strain showed on both sides. Dissent and desertion grew, and each government faced the question of how far it could go to win. You will read Lincoln’s own answer in the Gettysburg Address.
