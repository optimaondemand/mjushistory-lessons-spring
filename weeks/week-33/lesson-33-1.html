Lesson 33.1
Vicksburg and Gettysburg Turn the Tide
Lesson · ~830 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

casualties (KAZH-oo-ul-teez): Soldiers who are killed, wounded, captured, or missing in battle.

siege (SEEJ): A long attack in which an army surrounds a place and cuts off its food and supplies until it gives up.

📖 Today's Focus
Why do historians call the summer of 1863 the turning point of the war?

By the spring of 1863, the Confederacy had won big battles in Virginia, but it was running short of men and supplies. Lee made a bold plan to win the war by invading the North. At the same time, Grant was closing in on the last Confederate fortress on the Mississippi. In four days in July, both plans came to a head.

Lee Marches North

In May 1863 Lee won what many call his greatest victory, at Chancellorsville in Virginia. He split his smaller army, sent Jackson on a daring march around the Union flank, and routed a much larger force. But the victory had a terrible cost. That night, Jackson was accidentally shot by his own men. He died eight days later. Lee said he had lost his right arm.

Lee decided to invade the North again. Virginia’s farms were worn out, and a Northern invasion could feed his army on enemy land. It might also win foreign help, and make Northerners tired of the war. In June he marched his army into Pennsylvania.

Gettysburg

On June 28, Lincoln gave George Meade command of the Army of the Potomac, three days before the fighting began. The two armies met by chance at the town of Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, on July 1. Lee’s cavalry commander, J.E.B. Stuart, had ridden off on a long raid, so Lee did not know where the enemy was. The Confederates drove the Union soldiers through the town. But the Union troops took high ground south of it, on a ridge shaped like a fishhook.

On July 2, Lee attacked both ends of the Union line. At a hill called Little Round Top, Colonel Joshua Chamberlain and the 20th Maine held the end of the line against attack after attack. When their ammunition ran out, they charged downhill with bayonets. The Union line held.

On July 3, Lee made a famous decision. He believed his men could break the center of the Union line. About 12,500 soldiers, led by General George Pickett and others, marched across nearly a mile of open field. Union cannon and rifles tore them apart. About half of them were killed, wounded, or captured. Lee met the survivors and said, “It is all my fault.” The next day he began to retreat. The three days cost roughly 50,000 casualties. Lee never invaded the North again.

Vicksburg

While Lee marched north, Grant had been working for months to take Vicksburg, Mississippi. The city stood on high bluffs above the river, and its cannon blocked Union ships. In the spring Grant did something bold. He marched his army down the west bank, crossed the river below the city, and moved inland, living off the land. After winning several battles, he trapped the Confederate army inside the city and began a siege. For weeks, people in Vicksburg lived in caves dug into the hillsides to escape the shells. They ate mules and rats.

On July 4, 1863, the day after Pickett’s Charge, General John Pemberton surrendered Vicksburg, along with about 29,000 soldiers. A few days later the last Confederate fort on the river gave up. Lincoln wrote, “The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea.” The Confederacy was cut in two. Texas, Arkansas, and most of Louisiana were cut off from the rest.

Chattanooga

In the fall Grant won another victory. Confederate forces had trapped a Union army at Chattanooga, Tennessee, a key railroad town. In late November, Grant arrived, opened supply lines, and drove the Confederates off Missionary Ridge. The road to Georgia and the heart of the South was now open.

The Gettysburg Address

In November 1863 a new cemetery was dedicated at Gettysburg for the Union dead. The main speaker, Edward Everett, spoke for two hours. Then Lincoln stood and spoke for about two minutes. His speech had only about 270 words, and you will read it as your first primary source.

Lincoln began with the words “Four score and seven years ago,” meaning 87 years, which takes you back to 1776. He said the nation was founded on the idea that “all men are created equal.” He said the soldiers who died there had given their lives so that the nation would have “a new birth of freedom,” and so that government “of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth.” He did not talk about winning or losing. He talked about what the war was for.

Why 1863 Was a Turning Point

After July 1863 the Confederacy could no longer hope to win by invading the North or by outside help. It still had armies and a strong will to fight. But it was shrinking. The Union now held the Mississippi, and its armies were growing stronger. The cost was enormous. The war would be decided by exhaustion or by conquest, and it was not close to over.
