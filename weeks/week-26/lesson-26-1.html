Lesson 26.1
Southerners Take Texas
Lesson · ~820 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

annexation (an-ek-SAY-shun): Adding a territory to a country.

empresario (em-preh-SAH-ree-oh): A man who got a land grant from the Mexican government in return for bringing in settlers.

📖 Today's Focus
How did Mexican Texas become a slaveholding republic?

A cotton economy needs new land. By the 1820s, the best cotton land in Georgia and Alabama was filling up, and planters looked west. Just across the Sabine River lay Texas, a thinly settled part of Mexico with rich soil and rivers. This lesson tells how American settlers brought slavery into Texas, and what happened when Mexico tried to stop them.

Moses Austin’s Plan

Spain ruled Texas for centuries but never settled it in large numbers. Spain wanted settlers who would act as a buffer against raiding Comanche. In 1820 Moses Austin, a Missouri businessman, got permission to bring American families into Texas. He died soon after. His son, Stephen F. Austin, carried out the plan.

In 1821 Mexico won its independence from Spain and took over Texas. Mexico confirmed the Austin grant. Stephen F. Austin became an empresario. He brought about three hundred families, the “Old Three Hundred,” and more soon followed. The settlers had to promise to follow Mexican law and, on paper, to become Catholic. Most came from the South, and many brought enslaved people with them. Texas was not empty. The Comanche ruled the southern plains and raided settlements for horses and captives. As American families moved in, the Comanche and other Native peoples lost hunting grounds, and conflicts over land grew.

Slavery Becomes the Issue

Mexico had its own views about slavery. It had ended the slave trade, and it moved toward ending slavery itself. By 1829 Mexico had made slavery illegal across the country. Texas slaveholders objected, and for a time Mexico exempted Texas. Settlers also found ways around the law. They signed so-called labor contracts that kept people in bondage for life.

The Mexican government grew alarmed. Americans now outnumbered Mexicans in Texas by a wide margin. By 1835 about 30,000 settlers from the United States lived there, several thousand of them enslaved, while only a few thousand Tejanos did. Mexico feared that Texas would slip away from it, just as Florida had slipped away from Spain. In 1830 Mexico tried to stop further American immigration. The two sides grew more suspicious of each other. Stephen F. Austin, who had tried hard to keep peace, went to Mexico City in 1833 and was jailed for months. He came home convinced that Texas should be free of Mexico. From the Mexican side, the story looked different. Mexico had invited settlers in, and many repaid it with rebellion, bringing slavery along with them.

The Texas Revolution

Mexico was also changing. In 1834 its president, a general named Antonio López de Santa Anna, set aside the federal constitution that gave states their power and made himself a strong ruler. Many Texans, including some Mexican-born Texans called Tejanos, opposed him. Fighting began in October 1835.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Santa Anna (SAN-tah AH-nah): Antonio López de Santa Anna, the president of Mexico during the Texas Revolution.

Tejano (teh-HAH-no): A Texan of Mexican descent. The word means “Texan” in Spanish.

Texans declared independence on March 2, 1836. Four days later Santa Anna’s army overran the Alamo, an old mission in San Antonio, and killed all the defenders, including William Travis, Jim Bowie, and Davy Crockett. Weeks later at Goliad, Mexican troops executed hundreds of Texan prisoners. “Remember the Alamo” and “Remember Goliad” became rallying cries. Texas volunteers poured in from the United States, many of them from the South. On April 21, Sam Houston’s army surprised Santa Anna at San Jacinto and defeated him in about eighteen minutes. Santa Anna was captured and forced to sign an agreement recognizing Texas’s independence, though Mexico never accepted it.

Tejanos fought on the Texan side, including Juan Seguín and José Antonio Navarro, who signed the Declaration of Independence. After the war, many Tejanos lost land and respect in the republic they helped create.

A Slaveholding Republic

The Republic of Texas wrote a constitution in 1836. It protected slavery, and it barred free Black people from living there without special permission. Sam Houston became the first president. Houston had grown up in Tennessee, lived for years among the Cherokee, and served as governor of Tennessee. He was a friend of Andrew Jackson, and he thought Texas belonged in the Union. The number of enslaved people in Texas grew quickly in the years that followed. Southern planters saw Texas as a perfect place for cotton, with wide rivers and rich black soil, and they came by the thousands.

Texas wanted annexation, which means becoming part of the United States. In 1837 President Andrew Jackson recognized the new republic. But his successor, Martin Van Buren, did not want the fight that annexation would bring. Adding Texas would add a huge slave state. It would also risk war with Mexico. Texas remained an independent nation, a slaveholding republic next door.

💡 What If?
If Mexico had been able to enforce its ban on slavery and on American immigration, would Texas have stayed Mexican? Or would settlers still have rebelled? Historians disagree. Notice how small choices, such as Austin’s plan and Santa Anna’s centralization, shaped the outcome.
