📖 Today's Focus
Why did a speech by a man who lost an election become one of the most famous in American history?

By 1858 the nation had a new compromise that had failed, a bloody territory, and a Court decision that many Northerners could not accept. Into this scene walked Abraham Lincoln. He was a lawyer from Illinois, tall, plain-spoken, and almost unknown outside his state. He lost the election he ran in that year, and he still came out of it a national figure.

A Man Drawn Back

People who knew Lincoln said he was patient and hard-working. As a lawyer he rode a circuit of courts across Illinois, and he often told a story to make a point. These habits made him good at explaining hard questions in simple words. Lincoln was born in Kentucky in 1809 and grew up on frontier farms in Indiana and Illinois. He had little schooling and taught himself by reading. He became a lawyer, served one term in Congress, and then went back to his law practice. In 1854 the Kansas-Nebraska Act pulled him back into politics. He said the repeal of the Missouri Compromise was wrong because it let slavery spread to places where it had been forbidden. He joined the new Republican Party in 1856.

Lincoln was not an abolitionist like Garrison, who wanted slavery ended everywhere at once. He believed the Constitution protected slavery where it already existed, in the states. But he believed slavery was wrong. He wanted to stop it from spreading and to place it, in his words, on a course of “ultimate extinction,” meaning slow death over a long time.

Dred Scott and the Fear of a Plan

The Dred Scott decision changed things. If Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, then what could stop it from spreading? Lincoln feared the next step. Some slaveholders might argue that Northern states also could not keep slavery out. Lincoln said he would accept the decision as binding on Dred Scott and the parties to the case, but he would work to see it overturned by political action. That is how a free republic changes a bad law, through votes and debate, and not through violence.

The House Divided

In 1858 Lincoln ran for the U.S. Senate against Douglas. At that time state legislatures chose senators, so voters in effect chose legislators who promised to vote for one man or the other. On June 16, in Springfield, Lincoln accepted the Republican nomination with a speech that began with words from the Bible:

“A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved. I do not expect the house to fall. But I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other.”

Notice what Lincoln was saying. He did not say the Union would break. He said that the country could not go on forever with two opposite systems. Either slavery would spread everywhere, or it would be put on the road to ending. Many people thought the speech was too bold. Some of his friends thought it would cost him the election. Lincoln said that if it cost him the election, it was a truth that needed saying.

The Great Debates

Lincoln challenged Douglas to debate. They met seven times in towns across Illinois from August to October, and crowds of thousands came. Douglas said the voters should decide on slavery by popular sovereignty, and that Lincoln was a radical. Lincoln said the real question was whether slavery was wrong and ought to be stopped from spreading, and that Douglas refused to say it was wrong.

At the town of Freeport, Lincoln asked a sharp question. The Supreme Court said slaveholders could take slaves into any territory. How could settlers keep slavery out by popular sovereignty? Douglas answered that slavery needs local laws to protect it. If settlers refused to pass those laws, slavery could not survive. This answer is called the Freeport doctrine. It helped Douglas win his Illinois race. But it made many Southern Democrats distrust him, because it seemed to say the Court’s ruling could be ignored.

Lost the Race, Won the Stage

Douglas won. His party kept control of the Illinois legislature, and it sent him back to the Senate. But the debates were printed in newspapers around the country. Lincoln was now a national figure with a clear position. He also saw how the Democratic Party was cracking. Northern Democrats followed Douglas. Southern Democrats now wanted someone who would protect slavery in every territory.

Here was another moment when events might have gone differently. If Douglas had not given the Freeport answer, Southern Democrats might have stood with him in 1860, and the party might not have split. Instead, the one party that still reached across the sections began to divide. Lincoln had said aloud that the Union could not stay half slave and half free.
