Lesson 29.1
Douglas Attempts a New Compromise
Lesson · ~870 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

chief justice (CHEEF JUS-tis): The leading judge of the Supreme Court.

repeal (rih-PEEL): To cancel a law so that it no longer applies.

📖 Today's Focus
What happens when a politician tries to end a quarrel by reopening an old settlement?

Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois was short, quick, and full of energy. People called him the “Little Giant.” He believed the nation’s future lay in building, not in arguing over slavery. In 1854 he tried to clear the way for a railroad to the Pacific, and the plan ended with the Missouri Compromise destroyed and blood spilled in Kansas.

A Railroad and a Price

Douglas wanted a railroad to link Chicago to the Pacific coast. To build it, the land west of Missouri and Iowa had to be organized into territories with governments and laws. Southern senators had their own ideas about where the railroad should go. They also noticed something else. Under the Missouri Compromise of 1820, slavery was forbidden in this whole region, north of the 36°30′ line. Southern senators would not vote to organize it unless the ban came off.

Douglas agreed. His Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 created two new territories, Kansas and Nebraska. It said the settlers in each would decide the slavery question by popular sovereignty, which means a vote of the people living there. It also repealed the Missouri Compromise ban. President Franklin Pierce signed it. Douglas probably thought the settlers would keep slavery out of Nebraska on their own, and he did not seem to expect how Northerners would react.

The North Erupts

Douglas later said he was burned in effigy in so many towns that he could travel across the North by the light of his own burning image. For thirty-four years, the Missouri Compromise had been treated as a promise. Many Northerners felt it had been broken to help slaveholders. Whigs, Free Soilers, and antislavery Democrats formed a new party in 1854. They called it the Republican Party, and it had one main goal: stop slavery from spreading into the territories. The old Whig Party fell apart. Now there was a major political party that only existed in the North.

Remember George Washington’s Farewell Address, which warned against parties built on sections of the country. Here was just such a party, and it would soon win national power.

Bleeding Kansas

Kansas became the test of popular sovereignty. Both sides rushed settlers in. When the first elections came, thousands of Missourians crossed the border to vote, and a proslavery government took power. Free-state settlers refused to accept it and set up their own government in Topeka. For a time, Kansas had two governments and no peace.

In May 1856 a proslavery force attacked the free-state town of Lawrence. Days later, an abolitionist named John Brown led a night raid that killed five proslavery settlers along Pottawatomie Creek. You will meet Brown again in Module 30. Violence in the territory lasted for years, and dozens of people died. People began calling the place “Bleeding Kansas.” It was the first place where the nation’s quarrel turned into fighting between neighbors, and each side blamed the other.

The violence reached the Senate itself. After Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts gave a harsh speech about Kansas, Congressman Preston Brooks of South Carolina beat him with a cane on the Senate floor. Sumner needed years to recover. Northerners saw Brooks as a brute. Many Southerners sent Brooks new canes to show their support. The two sections no longer saw the same events in the same way.

The Court Steps In

Many Americans hoped the Supreme Court could end the argument. In 1857 it took up the case of a Missouri slave named Dred Scott. His master, an army doctor, had taken him to live for a time in Illinois, a free state, and in what is now Minnesota, a place where the Missouri Compromise banned slavery. After his master died, Scott sued for his freedom, arguing that living on free soil had made him free.

The case reached the Supreme Court. chief justice Roger Taney of Maryland wrote the opinion. The Court decided against Scott by a vote of 7 to 2. Taney wrote that no Black person, free or slave, could be a citizen of the United States and so could not sue in federal court. He also wrote that Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories. That meant the Missouri Compromise had always been unconstitutional.

You will read part of Taney’s opinion. Southerners were pleased. They thought the Court had settled the question in their favor. The North was shocked. Republicans said the decision made their entire program illegal, because their whole purpose was to keep slavery out of the territories. The Court had tried to settle what Congress could not, and instead it made the fight worse.

What Douglas Did Not See

Douglas was a skilled statesman with a gift for finding a deal. But his deals ran into a problem. He treated slavery as a question of procedure, to be settled by a vote. Many Northerners and many Southerners treated it as a question of right and wrong, and they would not accept a vote. His compromise failed to calm the South, angered the North, and ended in bloodshed. Douglas had hoped to be president one day, but now many in both sections distrusted him.
