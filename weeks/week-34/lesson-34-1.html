Lesson 6.7
Grant and Sherman Break the Confederacy
Lesson · ~840 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

forage (FOR-ij): To search the countryside for food and supplies.

total war (TOH-tul wor): War aimed at an enemy’s farms, railroads, and factories as well as its armies.

📖 Today's Focus
How did the Union finally break the Confederacy’s will to fight?

By 1864 the Union held the Mississippi, the seas, and much of the border. But Lee’s army was still undefeated, and the Confederacy still had its heartland. Lincoln needed a general who would use all of the North’s strength, and he needed to win an election in the middle of the war. This lesson follows both stories.

A New General-in-Chief

In March 1864 Lincoln named Ulysses S. Grant lieutenant general and put him in charge of all Union armies. He was the first full lieutenant general since George Washington. Grant made a plan that was simple and demanding. Every Union army would attack at the same time, so the Confederates could not shift troops from one place to another. Grant would travel with the Army of the Potomac and go after Lee. William T. Sherman would take an army into Georgia.

The Overland Campaign

In May Grant crossed the Rapidan River and fought Lee in a tangled forest called the Wilderness. After two days of terrible fighting, neither side had won. Earlier Union generals would have gone back to rest. Grant did the opposite. He turned his army south, around Lee’s flank, and kept going. Soldiers cheered when they saw which way they were headed.

For six weeks the two armies fought almost every day, at Spotsylvania and Cold Harbor and many other places. At Cold Harbor on June 3, Grant ordered an attack on strong trenches. In less than an hour thousands of Union soldiers fell. Grant later said he regretted it. In six weeks the Union lost about 55,000 men, and Lee lost about 33,000. But the Union could replace its men and the Confederacy could not.

Grant then moved his army across the James River and attacked the railroad town of Petersburg, south of Richmond. When the first attack failed, the armies dug in. The siege of Petersburg lasted nine months. Lee was pinned down, and his supply lines were being cut one by one.

Atlanta

In Georgia, Sherman pushed toward Atlanta, a key railroad and factory center. Confederate General Joseph Johnston fell back from position to position. President Davis grew impatient and replaced him with a bolder general, John Bell Hood. Hood attacked and lost many men. On September 2, 1864, Sherman telegraphed Lincoln that Atlanta was won.

An Election in Wartime

Atlanta came at the right time for Lincoln. That summer, with Grant’s heavy losses and no end in sight, Northern voters grew tired. Lincoln himself believed he would probably lose. His opponent was General George McClellan, running as a Democrat. The party platform called the war a failure and demanded peace, but McClellan said he would not accept peace without the Union.

The fall of Atlanta changed the mood. On November 8, Lincoln won all but three states and received 212 electoral votes to McClellan’s 21. Most soldiers voted for him. The country held a national election in the middle of a civil war, which few nations have done. It showed a republic with a strong civic habit of resolving disputes by vote, even in a crisis. It also meant that voters had approved of both the Union and emancipation.

The March to the Sea

In November Sherman left Atlanta with about 60,000 men and cut himself off from his supply lines. Over the next five weeks he marched about 285 miles to the coast at Savannah. His soldiers destroyed railroads by heating the rails and twisting them around trees. They would forage, and they took crops, livestock, and sometimes burned barns, mills, and cotton gins. They did not usually kill civilians, but the path of damage was about sixty miles wide. In the fall General Philip Sheridan had also burned barns and mills in the Shenandoah Valley, the Confederacy’s breadbasket. Sherman offered Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift. Thousands of freed people followed his army.

Sherman’s Idea of War

Sherman believed in total war. He thought that the Confederacy could not be beaten by armies alone, because its farms and railroads fed and supplied those armies, and because the people at home kept up the will to fight. He wrote that “war is cruelty, and you cannot refine it.” He wanted Southerners to see that rebellion would cost them everything, so they would end it sooner. Sherman argued that a harsh war was a shorter war, and in the long run a more merciful one.

Others disagreed. Some then and since have said that destroying private property and hurting civilians went too far. You will decide for yourself in the discussion for these lessons. Think about what Sherman did, what he did not do, and what he believed he was trying to accomplish.

What Remained

By early 1865 the Confederacy’s economy and armies were broken. But Lee’s army was still in the field. Lincoln’s reelection had shown that the North would stay with the war until the end. What the peace would look like, and what would happen to four million freed people, was still unknown.
