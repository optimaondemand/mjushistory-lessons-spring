blockade (blok-AYD): The use of warships to keep ships from entering or leaving an enemy’s ports.

border states (BOR-der stayts): The four slave states that stayed in the Union: Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri.

militia (mih-LISH-uh): A force of citizens who serve as soldiers for a limited time, usually called up by a state.

Union (YOON-yun): In the Civil War, the United States government and the states that stayed loyal to it.

📖 Today's Focus
How did the war begin, and which side held the advantages when it did?

Seven states had left the Union, but no shots had been fired. The new president had to decide what to do about federal forts inside the Confederacy. His choice would decide who started the war. In this lesson you will follow the crisis at Fort Sumter and see how the country divided into two camps.

A Cautious Inaugural

Abraham Lincoln took office on March 4, 1861, and gave a careful speech. He said he would not attack slavery where it already existed. He said he would “hold, occupy, and possess” federal property in the South. And he told the South: “You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors.” He ended by asking Americans to remember the “mystic chords of memory” that tied them together. You will read this speech.

The Fort in the Harbor

Fort Sumter stood on an island in the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina. It was held by about eighty-five U.S. soldiers under Major Robert Anderson. Their food was almost gone. Lincoln’s advisers disagreed about what to do. Some told him to give up the fort. Others said to send in troops by force.

Lincoln chose a narrow path. He sent ships carrying only food, not weapons or soldiers, and he told South Carolina’s governor they were coming. That put the choice in Jefferson Davis’s hands. If Davis allowed the ships through, the Union flag would still fly over a fort in Charleston. If he fired on a ship carrying bread, the Confederacy would be the one to start a war.

Davis ordered the fort taken. At 4:30 on the morning of April 12, 1861, Confederate cannon opened fire. The bombardment lasted about thirty-four hours. No one on either side was killed in the fighting, and Anderson surrendered on April 13. The war had begun.

Lincoln Calls for Troops

On April 15, Lincoln asked the states for 75,000 militia to serve for ninety days. The call changed the Upper South. Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas had refused to leave the Union as long as there was no fighting. Now they were asked to send troops against their neighbors. They would not. Virginia left on April 17, Arkansas on May 6, North Carolina on May 20, and Tennessee in June. Four more states joined the Confederacy, and its capital moved to Richmond, Virginia, only about a hundred miles from Washington.

Four slave states stayed in the Union. Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri were the border states. Lincoln worked hard to keep them. Losing Maryland would have cut off Washington, D.C. Kentucky controlled the Ohio River, and a loss there would have been very hard to undo.

Two Sides, Two Advantages

Most people expected a short war. They were wrong. The Union had far more people, about twenty-two million, compared with nine million in the Confederacy. And of those nine million, more than three million were slaves. The North made most of the country’s weapons and cloth and had most of its railroads. It had a navy, and it had the money to pay for a big war.

The Confederacy had advantages of its own. It was fighting a defensive war, so it only had to hold on. The Union had to invade a region the size of Western Europe and defeat its armies. Many of the country’s best officers, including Robert E. Lee and Thomas Jackson, went with the South. Southern soldiers also knew the land and were defending their homes. And Jefferson Davis hoped that Britain and France, which needed Southern cotton, would help the Confederacy.

The Confederacy also had a weakness. Its leaders had left the Union in the name of the rights of states. Now Davis needed those same states to hand over soldiers and money. Some governors refused. A government that cannot gather its own strength is in danger in wartime.

The Anaconda Plan

General Winfield Scott, the old hero of the Mexican War, offered a plan. The Union would use a blockade to close the Southern ports, and then take the Mississippi River to split the Confederacy in two. A big snake squeezes its prey slowly, so reporters called it the Anaconda Plan. Many people laughed at it because they wanted a quick victory. In time, the Union followed much of it.

First Bull Run

In July 1861 the Northern public pushed for action. An army of about 35,000 marched out of Washington toward Manassas Junction in Virginia, near a creek called Bull Run. Some people from Washington came along in carriages to watch, with picnic baskets. On July 21 the Union attack nearly worked. Then Confederate troops under Thomas Jackson held a hill. A general pointed and said, “There stands Jackson like a stone wall.” From then on he was called Stonewall Jackson.

Confederate reinforcements arrived by railroad, and the Union army broke and fled to Washington. Everyone now saw that the war would be long and hard. At this point the North was fighting only to keep the Union together, not to end slavery. In the next lesson you will see how the Union’s army in the East struggled to win.
