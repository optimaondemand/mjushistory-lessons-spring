Lesson 6.2
Lee and Jackson Outmaneuver the Army of the Potomac
Lesson · ~840 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

campaign (kam-PAYN): A series of battles and marches that aims at one military goal.

flank (FLANGK): The side of an army. To attack the flank is to hit it from the side, where it is weakest.

peninsula (peh-NIN-suh-luh): A strip of land with water on three sides.

📖 Today's Focus
What happens when a big army has a general who will not use it?

After Bull Run, Lincoln knew he needed a better army and a better general. He found a man who could build the army. He could not find one who would use it. Meanwhile, the Confederates in Virginia had two of the boldest commanders in American history. This lesson follows the fighting in the East during 1861 and 1862.

Two Capitals, One Hundred Miles Apart

Washington and Richmond were only about a hundred miles apart. Each side had to protect its own capital and wanted to take the other. That is why so many of the war’s early battles were fought in the same small part of Virginia. The Union army in the East, called the Army of the Potomac after the river near Washington, faced the Confederate army defending Richmond.

A General Who Builds an Army

Lincoln put thirty-four-year-old George B. McClellan in charge of the Union’s main army, the Army of the Potomac. McClellan was an excellent organizer. He trained the soldiers, fed them well, and gave them pride, and they loved him. But McClellan had a serious flaw. He always believed the enemy was much stronger than it really was, so he was afraid to attack. He relied on a detective agency whose reports counted Confederate soldiers and always came up with numbers far too high, and McClellan believed them. He also treated Lincoln with scorn. Lincoln once said that if McClellan did not want to use the army, he would like to borrow it.

The Peninsula Campaign

In the spring of 1862, McClellan took more than 100,000 men by ship to Virginia. He landed on a peninsula between two rivers and began to march toward Richmond. Confederate General Joseph Johnston fell back, and by late May McClellan was close enough to see the church spires of the capital.

Then two things happened. First, Johnston was badly wounded in a battle at Seven Pines. Second, Jefferson Davis put Robert E. Lee in his place. This was a turning point. Had Johnston not been wounded, Lee might never have led the army. Lee renamed it the Army of Northern Virginia.

Jackson in the Valley

Lee had help from Thomas “Stonewall” Jackson, a stern, deeply religious man who had taught at the Virginia Military Institute before the war. In the spring of 1862 Jackson led about 17,000 men through the Shenandoah Valley, a long green valley in western Virginia, in what is called his Valley campaign. In forty-eight days his men marched more than 600 miles. Moving fast, they won a string of battles against Union forces that together were far larger than his own. The Union had to keep tens of thousands of soldiers near Washington to guard against him. Those soldiers were kept away from McClellan.

Lee Attacks

Lee was fifty-five years old. He had graduated near the top of his class at West Point and served with courage in the Mexican War. In 1861 Scott had offered him command of the Union army, but Lee refused. He said he could not raise his hand against his native Virginia.

In late June, Lee struck. In a week of fighting called the Seven Days, he attacked McClellan again and again. Lee lost more men than McClellan, because his attacks were bold and costly. But McClellan lost his nerve. Despite having a larger army and winning some of the fights, he pulled back to the James River. About 36,000 men were killed, wounded, or missing in just one week, and Richmond was saved.

Second Bull Run

Lincoln put General John Pope in charge of a new army in northern Virginia, and he ordered McClellan’s men to join it. In August, Lee sent Jackson on a long march around Pope’s flank. Jackson captured a huge supply base at Manassas. When Pope turned to attack, he walked into a trap. On August 29–30, on the same ground as First Bull Run, Lee’s army drove the Union army from the field. It was another humiliating defeat. Lincoln had few choices left. He put McClellan back in command of the troops around Washington, because no one else could rebuild the army’s spirit so quickly.

Virtue and Failure

Why did the smaller army keep winning? Part of the answer is a matter of character. Lee had courage and a willingness to take great risks. He trusted his officers to act on their own, and Jackson’s speed and boldness fit this style. McClellan had a very different habit. He feared losing his army more than he wanted to win. That trait is what made his great skill at organizing so costly. Lincoln did not have a general who would fight.

By the end of the summer, the Union had lost almost everywhere in the East. The Confederates held Richmond, and Lee decided to take the war north. He crossed into Maryland in early September. That decision would lead to the battle of Antietam, which you will study in Lesson 6.4, and it would change the war’s purpose.
