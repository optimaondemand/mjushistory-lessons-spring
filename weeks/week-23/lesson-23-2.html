📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

predestination (pree-des-tih-NAY-shun): The belief that God decided long ago who will be saved, so people cannot change it.

revival (rih-VY-vul): A series of religious meetings meant to stir up people’s faith.

temperance (TEM-per-ens): Using little or no alcohol. The temperance movement tried to persuade people to give up drinking.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did so many Americans turn to religion just as the country grew richer?

New machines and new money changed daily life fast. Many Americans moved to cities or to the frontier, away from the churches where they grew up. Some preachers worried that the nation was growing rich but not good. They set out to wake people up. The result is called the Second Great Awakening, and it changed American life as much as any machine did.

Looking Back: The First Awakening

You may remember an earlier wave of religious excitement from Fall. In the 1730s and 1740s, preachers such as Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield drew huge crowds in the colonies. That was the First Great Awakening. Now, about sixty years later, a second wave arrived. It was bigger, it reached more of the country, and it did more to change American society.

Cane Ridge and the Frontier

In August 1801 thousands of people gathered at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, for a camp meeting. Estimates run from ten thousand to twenty thousand. Families came in wagons and camped for days. Preachers from several churches, including Presbyterians, Methodists, and Baptists, spoke at the same time in different parts of the field. People wept, prayed, and sang through the night.

Camp meetings spread across the frontier. Methodist circuit riders traveled on horseback from settlement to settlement, preaching in cabins and fields wherever people lived. A frontier far from established churches became full of revival religion. A revival is a series of meetings meant to stir up faith. Slaves often attended these meetings too, usually in separate sections. Many took Christianity as their own. In the Bible’s story of the Exodus, they heard that God cares about people who are held in bondage.

Charles Finney and a Message of Choice

The most famous preacher of the Second Awakening was Charles Finney. He was a lawyer who had a religious experience in 1821 and became a preacher. His revivals in western New York, especially in Rochester in 1830 and 1831, were so powerful that the region was nicknamed the “burned-over district.” Revival fires had swept through it again and again.

Finney broke with the older teaching of many Calvinist churches. They held to predestination: God had already decided who would be saved. Finney said that every person could choose to turn to God. He urged listeners to act at once. He let women pray aloud in mixed meetings, which shocked many people. He also said that a person who was saved should work to improve society. Lyman Beecher, a minister in Boston and Connecticut, preached the same message of duty. He was also the father of Harriet Beecher Stowe, whom you will meet later.

Voluntary Societies

Revival religion did not stay inside church walls. Those who were stirred by it formed voluntary societies, groups that people joined by choice to fix a problem. There were societies to print Bibles, to start Sunday schools, to send missionaries, and to promote temperance. Many members of these groups were women, who could not vote or hold office but could organize and raise money. The temperance movement had real results. Americans drank far less alcohol in 1850 than in 1830, by some estimates less than half as much. Black Americans built their own churches, too. In 1816 Richard Allen and others founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia.

A French visitor named Alexis de Tocqueville traveled the United States in the early 1830s. He noticed that Americans joined groups to solve problems together instead of waiting for the government. He thought this habit helped explain why American democracy worked. Habits like these, a love for fellow citizens beyond one’s family and a willingness to cooperate, are exactly what a free republic needs.

A New Church

The burned-over district also produced a new religion. In 1830 Joseph Smith, a young man from western New York, published the Book of Mormon and founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The church grew quickly, and its members were often treated harshly. They moved from New York to Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, and finally to Utah in 1847.

A Conscience, Awake and Divided

The Second Awakening built a mass evangelical culture in the North and on the frontier, and in much of the South too. But an awakened conscience asks hard questions. If every soul matters to God, is it right to own another person? Northerners and Southerners read the same Bible. Over the years they reached opposite answers. Some of the people who joined temperance societies would soon join antislavery societies. You will see where that led.
