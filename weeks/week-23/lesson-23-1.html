📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

interchangeable parts (in-ter-CHAYN-juh-bul PARTS): Parts made so exactly alike that any one of them fits any machine of its kind.

textile (TEKS-tile): Cloth, or anything woven or knitted. A textile mill is a factory that makes cloth.

wage labor (WAYJ LAY-ber): Working for pay, usually by the hour or the day, instead of working your own farm or shop.

📖 Today's Focus
How did machines, canals, and railroads change where and how Americans worked?

While Henry Clay argued about tariffs, the North was changing in a way no law had planned. Machines made goods faster and cheaper. Canals and steamboats made it cheaper to ship them. In a single lifetime, many Northerners went from making almost everything at home to buying much of what they used. This lesson shows how that change happened and what it cost.

Stealing a Secret

In the 1780s Britain led the world in making cloth with machines. British law forbade skilled workers to leave the country and forbade sending out plans of the machines. Samuel Slater, a young mill worker, memorized how the machines worked and slipped out of England in 1789. A Rhode Island merchant hired him. In 1790 Slater built a water-powered mill at Pawtucket. It spun cotton thread, the first step in making textiles. Slater hired whole families, and children as young as seven or eight tended the machines.

The Lowell Mills

Francis Cabot Lowell, a wealthy Boston merchant, toured British mills between 1810 and 1812. He memorized the design of a power loom, a machine that weaves thread into cloth. With partners, he built a mill at Waltham, Massachusetts, in 1813. It was the first in America to do every step, from raw cotton to finished cloth, under one roof. After Lowell died, his partners built a bigger mill town on the Merrimack River and named it Lowell.

The Lowell mills hired young farm women, many in their teens and twenties. They lived in company boardinghouses with strict rules, and they earned cash wages. For many it was the first money they had ever controlled. But the days were twelve or thirteen hours long, the machines were loud, and the air was full of lint. When owners cut pay in the 1830s, the women went on strike. One of them, Harriet Hanson Robinson, started working in the mills at age ten. Years later she wrote about what it was like.

Moving Goods Cheaply

Making goods was only half the problem. Moving them was the other half. Robert Fulton’s steamboat, the Clermont, traveled up the Hudson River in 1807, even against the current. Before steam, boats going upriver were slow and hard to manage. Steam power made rivers into highways in both directions.

The greatest project was the Erie Canal. Governor DeWitt Clinton pushed for it, and critics mocked it as “Clinton’s Ditch.” New York paid for it with its own money. Workers, many of them Irish immigrants, dug 363 miles by hand and with horses, from Albany to Buffalo. The canal opened in 1825. Shipping a ton of freight from Buffalo to New York City had cost about $100. Now it cost about $10. Cities such as Rochester and Buffalo boomed, and New York City became the nation’s greatest port.

Railroads came next. The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad began in 1830. By 1850 the country had more than 9,000 miles of track. Together, steamboats, canals, and railroads are called the transportation revolution.

Inventions That Changed Work

Eli Whitney promised the government muskets made with interchangeable parts. If every part were exactly alike, a broken part could be replaced from a box, and workers did not need to be master craftsmen. The idea took years to perfect, mostly in the federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry. Later, it spread to clocks, locks, and sewing machines.

Farm machines changed the countryside. Cyrus McCormick’s reaper, built in the 1830s, cut grain far faster than a man with a hand tool. In 1837 John Deere made a steel plow that could slice through the sticky soil of the Midwest prairie. These tools made the Midwest attractive to farmers who wanted to own land and sell crops, not to work in a factory. In 1844 Samuel Morse sent the first long-distance telegraph message, from Washington to Baltimore: “What hath God wrought.” News that once took days now took minutes.

A Market Revolution

Historians call all of this the market revolution. Early in the century, most Northern families grew or made most of what they used. Now farmers sold wheat and corn to distant cities and bought cloth, tools, and shoes. More people worked for wages instead of working their own land. Millions of immigrants, especially from Ireland and Germany, arrived to fill the jobs. The Irish dug canals, laid rails, and worked in mills. Many Germans became farmers in the Midwest.

Notice what was happening. Machines and canals did not appear by themselves. Inventors, investors, and state governments all made choices. But once the new tools existed, people had strong reasons to use them, and daily life changed. The North became one of the most productive economies in the world.

💡 Progress, and Its Price
The same changes that made the North rich pulled workers from farms into factories and widened the gap between the industrial North and the farming South. Notice something else. Those Lowell mills ran on cotton, and that cotton was grown in the South by slaves. The two sections were growing into very different economies, yet they were tied together.
