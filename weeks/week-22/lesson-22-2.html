📖 Today's Focus
Who counted as “the people” in the age of Andrew Jackson?

In the 1820s the United States became much more democratic. More men could vote, and more men did. A new kind of leader rose up, a general with a rough frontier background who spoke for ordinary voters. But democracy for some Americans came at a heavy cost to others. This lesson covers both.

More Voters

At first, most states required a man to own property before he could vote. The idea was that only property owners had a stake in the country. But new states in the West, like Indiana and Illinois, did not want those rules. They let nearly all white men vote. Older states then dropped their property rules, one by one. The growth of cities and factories added thousands of wage-earners, men who owned little but wanted a say in government.

This was the expansion of suffrage. It was large, but it was not equal. Women could not vote, and neither could Native Americans. In several states, free Black men who once could vote lost the right in these years. Democracy grew for white men while shrinking for some Black men.

The Corrupt Bargain

In 1824 four men ran for President. Andrew Jackson, a hero of New Orleans, won the most electoral and popular votes. But no one won a majority of electors, so the House had to decide. Henry Clay, the Speaker, backed John Quincy Adams. Adams won, and then he made Clay his Secretary of State. Jackson’s supporters shouted that a “corrupt bargain” had stolen the election. There was never proof of a deal. But the charge stuck.

Jackson spent four years organizing. In the election of 1828, the number of voters roughly tripled. Jackson won easily. He was a wealthy slaveholder, but he presented himself as a man of the people. Ordinary voters felt that this was their victory.

A New Kind of President

Jackson had been orphaned young and had grown up on the Tennessee frontier. He was brave, stubborn, and quick to anger, and he never forgot an insult. These traits helped him win at New Orleans. They also made him a fierce enemy and a President who did not like to be told no. The 1828 and 1832 campaigns were new in other ways. They had parades, barbecues, party newspapers, and by 1832 national party conventions to choose candidates. Jackson believed the President was the one official chosen by all the people, and he acted that way. He gave government jobs to his supporters, an approach called the spoils system. His friends said it gave new people a turn. Critics said it ignored skill.

Jackson also used the veto freely. He killed a bill to pay for a road in Kentucky, Clay’s home state. In 1832 he vetoed the bill to renew the Second Bank. He called it a tool of the rich. Clay had made the bank a campaign issue. Jackson won re-election by a landslide. His strong style made some Americans cheer. It made others worry that a President might claim too much power. That worry would grow when South Carolina challenged him.

Removal

Jackson believed Native nations in the South stood in the way of settlers. Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi wanted their land. The Cherokee had done everything the United States had asked. They had a written constitution, farms, and a newspaper. Sequoyah had created a writing system for their language. None of this stopped the removal.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Sequoyah (sih-KWOY-uh): The Cherokee man who created a written alphabet for the Cherokee language.

In 1830 Congress passed the Indian Removal Act by a very close vote in the House, and Jackson signed it. The law began the move of the southeastern nations west of the Mississippi. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), the Supreme Court said the Cherokee were a “domestic dependent nation” and refused to hear the case. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832), Marshall’s Court said Georgia had no right to rule Cherokee land. Georgia ignored the ruling, and Jackson did nothing to enforce it. The rule of law depends on leaders obeying courts they dislike.

A small group of Cherokee signed a removal treaty in 1835 without the approval of most of their nation. In 1838 soldiers forced about 16,000 Cherokee to march west on what became known as the Trail of Tears. Thousands died of cold, hunger, and disease.

In Florida, the Seminoles refused to move. The Second Seminole War lasted from 1835 to 1842 and was one of the longest and costliest Indian wars the United States ever fought. A leader named Osceola became famous for his resistance. Many Black Seminoles, who were free or had escaped slavery, fought beside the Seminoles because they would lose their freedom if the army won. Some Seminoles were removed. Others stayed in the Everglades.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Osceola (OSS-ee-OH-luh): A Seminole leader in the Second Seminole War.

Many Americans in this time spoke of the nation’s right to spread across the continent. The idea would not get its famous name, Manifest Destiny, until 1845. But it was already at work.

Democracy’s Two Faces

Jackson’s America widened the vote, put ordinary men into the political system, and made the presidency a voice for the people. It also removed Native nations and left slavery untouched. A historian of this period cannot honestly report only one of these. Both are true, and each shaped what came next. Jackson’s strong style would be tested by a challenge from South Carolina.
