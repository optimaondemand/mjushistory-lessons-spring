Lesson 21.1
The New Republic Competes with Colonial Powers in North America
Lesson · ~830 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

embargo (em-BAR-go): A government order that stops trade, often by keeping ships from leaving port.

expedition (ek-spih-DISH-un): A journey made for a purpose, such as exploring.

sovereignty (SOV-rin-tee): A nation’s right to rule itself without outside control.

📖 Today's Focus
How did a revolution in Haiti double the size of the United States?

Having decided how it would govern itself, the United States now had to hold its own against Britain, France, and Spain. Each had land on the same continent and each had a powerful navy. Britain was at the north and west. Spain held Florida and Texas. France held a huge claim to land beyond the Mississippi River. President Jefferson wanted to secure the Mississippi and keep Europe’s wars away from American shores.

Why Napoleon Sold Louisiana

In 1800, France got Louisiana back from Spain. Louisiana was a huge territory west of the Mississippi River. The United States depended on the river and on the port of New Orleans. Western farmers floated their crops down the river to sell them. If a strong power controlled New Orleans, the whole West could be choked off. Jefferson wrote that the nation that held it was “our natural and habitual enemy.”

Napoleon Bonaparte, the ruler of France, had big plans. He wanted to make Louisiana the supply base for a rich sugar colony in the Caribbean, Saint-Domingue, now Haiti. But there was a problem. In 1791, enslaved people there rose up against their French masters. Their leader was Toussaint Louverture, who had himself been enslaved. The revolt grew into a revolution, and it led to a new nation, Haiti, ruled by the people who had been enslaved.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Toussaint Louverture (too-SAHN loo-vair-TOOR): The leader of the revolution in Haiti, who had been enslaved.

Napoleon sent a large army to take back the colony. It failed. Yellow fever and fierce fighting killed tens of thousands of French soldiers. Napoleon’s own men captured Toussaint, who died in a French prison in 1803. But the army never won. Haiti declared itself independent on January 1, 1804. The United States, with its own enslaved population, refused to recognize it for nearly sixty years. Many Southern slaveholders feared that the revolt would inspire uprisings at home. Napoleon gave up on his plans for the Americas. Without Haiti, Louisiana had little value to him. He also needed money for war against Britain.

The Biggest Bargain in American History

In 1803 Jefferson sent James Monroe to Paris. His job was to buy New Orleans. Instead, the French offered to sell the whole of Louisiana. The price was $15 million. The United States got about 828,000 square miles. The country doubled in size, and the Mississippi was secure.

Here is a surprise. Jefferson had always argued that the federal government could use only powers the Constitution lists. Nothing in it said the President could buy land from a foreign country. Jefferson wondered whether he needed an amendment. In the end, he decided the chance was too good to lose. The Senate approved the treaty, 24 to 7. The man who feared stretching the Constitution stretched it. Sometimes events force leaders to bend their principles.

The purchase did not make the land empty. Many Native nations lived there, along with French and Spanish settlers and thousands of enslaved people. None of these people had been asked about the sale. Its future would raise a question that would shake the country: would slavery spread into the new land?

Lewis and Clark

Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore the new land. Their expedition left in 1804 and followed the Missouri River west. Jefferson hoped to find a water route to the Pacific. They did not, but they brought back maps, plants, and knowledge of the Native nations they met. Along the way, they depended on those nations for food, horses, and directions. The Mandan fed them through the winter of 1804–05, and the Nez Perce helped them after they crossed the Rocky Mountains.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Sacagawea (sah-KAH-gah-WEE-uh): A Shoshone woman who traveled with Lewis and Clark as a guide and interpreter.

The expedition was not only Lewis and Clark. A Shoshone woman named Sacagawea joined them with her baby son. She helped as an interpreter and guide, and her presence showed other nations that the group came in peace. York, an enslaved man owned by Clark, was a strong hunter and swimmer, and he was a valued member of the party. After the trip, Clark did not free York for years. The group reached the Pacific in November 1805.

Embargo and Frustration

While the United States was growing, Europe was at war. Britain and France seized American ships that traded with the other side. Jefferson did not want war. In 1807 he asked Congress for an embargo. The Embargo Act kept American ships from sailing to foreign ports. Jefferson hoped Europe would feel the pain and respect American sovereignty, the right to rule ourselves without outside control.

It did not work. Britain and France found other places to buy goods. The people who suffered were American sailors, merchants, and farmers, especially in New England. Congress ended the embargo in 1809. The country was still unprotected on the seas, and Britain was still arming Native nations in the Northwest. Spain still held Florida and Texas, and Britain claimed the Oregon country on the Pacific coast. The next lesson shows how the country drifted toward war.
