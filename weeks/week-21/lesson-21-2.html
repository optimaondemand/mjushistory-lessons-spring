Madison Wars with Britain; Monroe Warns Europe to Stay Out of the Western Hemisphere
Lesson · ~830 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

confederacy (kun-FED-er-uh-see): A group of nations or states joined together for a shared purpose.

doctrine (DOK-trin): An official statement of a government’s policy or principle.

impressment (im-PRESS-ment): Forcing men to serve in a navy.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did a poorly prepared republic go to war, and what did it gain?

Britain seized American sailors, armed Native resistance, and ignored American protests. By 1812 President James Madison, a thoughtful man but an untested war leader, had run out of ways to avoid war. This lesson covers the War of 1812 and the years after, when a country that had barely survived began to speak as if it were a power.

Sailors and Warriors

Britain’s navy needed men. It stopped American ships and took sailors off them, claiming they were British deserters. This is impressment. Thousands of men were taken, and some were Americans. In 1807 a British warship, HMS Leopard, fired on the American ship Chesapeake off the Virginia coast. Three Americans were killed, and four sailors were taken. Americans were furious.

At the same time, trouble was growing on the frontier. A Shawnee leader named Tecumseh and his brother, Tenskwatawa, called the Prophet, asked Native nations to unite. White settlers had pushed Native peoples off their land by treaties that many Native people said were unfair. Tecumseh said that no single nation could sell land that belonged to all. He built a confederacy of nations in the Northwest and in the South. In 1811, American troops under William Henry Harrison beat the Prophet’s followers at Tippecanoe. Tecumseh was away in the South, trying to win more allies, when the battle began. Had he been there, the outcome might have been different. Many Americans blamed the British for arming Tecumseh. The alliance grew stronger, and Tecumseh joined Britain when war broke out.

The War of 1812

Madison asked Congress for war in June 1812. The vote was close. The United States had a tiny army and a small navy, and its first invasions of Canada failed. Detroit surrendered. But American ships won some battles at sea, and in 1813 Oliver Perry won control of Lake Erie. Tecumseh was killed that October. His confederacy fell apart. After the war, Britain stopped helping Native nations in the Northwest. They had lost their most powerful ally, and settlers moved in faster than before.

In August 1814 the British marched into Washington and burned the Capitol and the President’s house. Dolley Madison saved a famous portrait of George Washington before she fled. A few weeks later, American defenders held Fort McHenry in Baltimore, which inspired the song that became “The Star-Spangled Banner.”

In the South, Andrew Jackson crushed a group of Creek warriors at Horseshoe Bend in 1814. The treaty that followed took about twenty million acres from the Creek, including from Creek who had fought on his side. Then, on January 8, 1815, Jackson’s men beat a much larger British army at New Orleans. The battle came after peace had been signed. The Treaty of Ghent, signed in December 1814, put everything back the way it was before the war. But news traveled slowly. To most Americans, New Orleans felt like proof that they had won.

Florida and a Treaty

Florida belonged to Spain. It was a refuge for Seminoles and for slaves who had escaped from the United States. Some had built free Black communities, and many allied with the Seminoles. Spanish and American settlers lived there too. In 1816 American troops destroyed a fort on the Apalachicola River held by free and escaped Black people and their Native allies. In 1818 Jackson led an army into Florida, saying he was chasing Seminole raiders. He seized Spanish forts, and he executed two British men.

Spain was weak, and it could not hold Florida. In 1819, John Quincy Adams, the Secretary of State, signed a treaty with Spain’s representative. The Adams–Onís Treaty gave Florida to the United States. In return, the United States gave up claims to Texas and agreed to pay up to $5 million in claims by its citizens against Spain. The line between the two countries now reached to the Pacific. Florida became a territory in 1821. It would stay a territory until 1845, and it would be shared by Seminoles, free Black residents, and Spanish and American settlers. Not all of them would be allowed to stay.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Adams–Onís (AD-umz oh-NEESE): The name of the 1819 treaty between the United States and Spain. Luis de Onís was Spain’s representative.

The Monroe Doctrine

In the early 1820s, the countries of Latin America won their independence from Spain. Some Americans worried that European kings would try to take them back. In December 1823, President James Monroe sent a message to Congress. Secretary of State John Quincy Adams helped shape it. It said two things. Europe must not start new colonies in the Americas. And the United States would stay out of Europe’s wars. This became known as the Monroe Doctrine.

It was a bold claim from a country that could not enforce it. The real power behind it was the British navy. Britain wanted free trade in the Americas and did not want other empires to have it. Still, the message set a goal for the future. The United States called the whole hemisphere its own concern.
