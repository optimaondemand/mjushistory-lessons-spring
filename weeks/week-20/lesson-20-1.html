📖 Today's Focus
Would the losers accept defeat? Would the winners govern with restraint?

By 1800 the Federalists and Republicans had become fiercely partisan. Each side hated and feared the other. Each side thought the other would ruin the country. In the history of republics, bitter rivals often ended up fighting each other. This lesson asks whether a republic can do something that looks simple but is very hard: lose an election and walk away.

A Tie Nobody Planned

In the election of 1800, the Republican candidates were Thomas Jefferson for President and Aaron Burr for Vice President. The Federalists backed John Adams. Each state’s legislature chose electors, and the electors voted. The Republicans won. But the Constitution, as it was then written, had each elector cast two votes for President with no separate vote for Vice President. All the Republican electors voted for both Jefferson and Burr. Both men got 73 votes.

No one had planned for a tie between two men on the same ticket. Under the Constitution, a tie went to the House of Representatives. Each state got one vote, and a candidate needed nine of sixteen states to win. Many Federalists in the House saw a last chance. They could keep Jefferson out by supporting Burr.

There was a second factor, a rule about counting. The Constitution sets each state’s number of seats in the House by its population, and a state’s electors are based on its seats. At the Constitutional Convention, Southern delegates wanted every slave counted in a state’s population, which would have given their states more seats. Many Northern delegates wanted none counted. The compromise was to count all free persons plus three-fifths of the slave population. It was a formula for dividing up seats and taxes among the states, not a statement about what any person was worth. Even so, the formula added seats and electors to the slaveholding states. Without those added electors, Jefferson might have lost to Adams.

Thirty-Six Votes

The House voted again and again. It took 36 ballots over about a week in February 1801. Some leaders talked about using force. Republican leaders in Virginia and Pennsylvania spoke of calling out their militias if the Federalists tried to take the presidency. The country was closer to a crisis than most people today realize.

Alexander Hamilton settled the matter. He disliked Jefferson’s ideas. But he thought Burr was dangerous because he had no principles. Hamilton wrote letters urging Federalists to support Jefferson. Enough of them gave way. Jefferson won on the 36th ballot. Hamilton put the republic ahead of his own party’s wishes. (Burr never forgave him. In 1804 Burr killed Hamilton in a duel.)

💡 Fixing the Flaw
The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, fixed the problem. Electors now cast one vote for President and a separate vote for Vice President. A tie between running mates could never happen again.

A Peaceful Handoff

On March 4, 1801, Jefferson took the oath of office. He was the first President to be inaugurated in Washington, D.C., which was still half built. John Adams had left the city that morning. He did not attend. It was not a graceful exit, but the important thing was that he left. The party that had held power for twelve years gave it up without a shot.

Jefferson’s speech tried to calm the country. He said that every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle. “We are all Republicans, we are all Federalists,” he said. Years later, Jefferson called the election of 1800 a revolution. He meant that it had changed the course of the nation by ballots, not by bullets.

Why does this matter? The rule of law means that rules, not strength, decide who rules. A republic cannot last unless the losers accept the result. In 1800 that was a young and untested habit. It would be tested again in 1860.

A Quiet Government

Jefferson wanted a government that was small, cheap, and close to the people. With his Treasury Secretary, Albert Gallatin, he cut spending and reduced the army and navy. The Republican Congress ended the whiskey tax and other inside taxes, so that most of the government’s money came from tariffs on imported goods. The national debt began to fall. Jefferson also let the Alien and Sedition Acts run out and pardoned those who had been convicted under the Sedition Act.

The government Jefferson led did not undo Hamilton’s work. The Bank stayed, and so did the debt plan. Jefferson learned something many leaders learn: it is easier to promise change than to unmake what is already working. Evaluate that choice for yourself. Was it wisdom, or was it a case of giving up on his principles? Historians disagree.

The Midnight Judges

Peace came with a catch. In the last weeks of his term, Adams and the Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801. It created new federal judgeships. Adams filled them with Federalists, working late into his last night in office, according to the stories told afterward. People called these men the “midnight judges.”

The Republicans were furious. They controlled Congress and the presidency, but the courts stayed in Federalist hands. In 1802 the Republican Congress voted to repeal the Act and abolish the new judgeships. The Federalists, who had lost both of the elected branches, still held the courts. The new party in power now had to deal with a Federalist judiciary. The fight between the two sides had changed from a fight over the presidency to a fight over who would decide what the Constitution meant. The next lesson tells how that fight turned out.
