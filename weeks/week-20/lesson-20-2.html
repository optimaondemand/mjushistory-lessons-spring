Lesson 20.2
Marshall Builds a Strong Court and a Strong Nation
Lesson · ~950 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

commerce (KOM-ers): Trade: buying and selling between people, states, or nations.

judicial review (joo-DISH-ul rih-VYOO): The Court’s power to decide that a law goes against the Constitution.

supremacy (soo-PREM-uh-see): Having the highest authority.

unconstitutional (un-kon-stih-TOO-shun-ul): Not allowed by the Constitution.

📖 Today's Focus
Who has the final word on what the Constitution means?

Jefferson’s party had won the presidency and Congress. But the Supreme Court was led by a Federalist, Chief Justice John Marshall. Marshall served for 34 years, from 1801 to 1835, and he made the Court far more powerful than most people expected. This lesson follows four of his most important decisions.

A Chief Justice Who Almost Wasn’t

John Adams did not first ask Marshall to be Chief Justice. He asked John Jay, who had held the job before. Jay said no. Only then did Adams turn to Marshall, who was his Secretary of State. Had Jay said yes, American law might look very different today. Marshall was a Virginian, a Revolutionary War veteran, and a gifted thinker. He was also a distant cousin and a political rival of Thomas Jefferson.

Marbury v. Madison (1803)

Remember the midnight judges. William Marbury was one of them. Adams had signed his commission as a justice of the peace, but the papers had not been delivered before Jefferson took office. Jefferson’s Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to deliver them. Marbury sued and asked the Supreme Court to order Madison to do it.

Marshall faced a trap. If he ordered Madison to deliver the papers, Jefferson could simply ignore the Court, and the Court would look weak. If he refused, he would seem to give in to Jefferson. His answer was clever. He said Marbury did have a right to the commission. But the law that gave the Court the power to order it, part of the Judiciary Act of 1789, went beyond what the Constitution allowed. He ruled it unconstitutional.

Marshall gave up a small power to claim a far bigger one. The Court had never before said that it could throw out an act of Congress. This power is called judicial review. Jefferson won the case, but Marshall won the argument. The Court was now an equal branch of government, with the right to say what the Constitution means. Jefferson did not like it. He believed that each branch of government, not just the Court, could judge what the Constitution allowed. Then something remarkable happened. The Court did not strike down another act of Congress for 54 years. That did not happen until the Dred Scott case in 1857.

Fletcher v. Peck (1810)

In 1810 the Court used its new power again, this time against a state. Georgia had sold huge pieces of land in a fraud scandal. Later, a new legislature tried to cancel the sales. The Court ruled that the cancellation broke the Constitution’s protection of contracts. It was the first time the Court struck down a state law. Marshall was saying that the Constitution limits the states as well as Congress.

McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

Remember Hamilton’s bank and the argument over implied powers? It came to the Supreme Court in 1819. Congress had created the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland tried to tax the Baltimore branch, a sure way to weaken it. James McCulloch, the cashier of the Baltimore branch, refused to pay.

Marshall’s Court sided with the bank. First, it said Congress could create a bank. The Constitution lets Congress do what is “necessary and proper” to carry out its listed powers, and Marshall read that clause as Hamilton had. Second, the Court said that a state could not tax a federal institution. Marshall noted that the power to tax can become the power to destroy. If a state could tax a federal bank, it could kill it. The Constitution and federal laws, he wrote, are the supreme law. This is federal supremacy.

Gibbons v. Ogden (1824)

New York had given one company the sole right to run steamboats on its waters. A rival, Thomas Gibbons, ran steamboats between New Jersey and New York under a federal license. The Court had to decide whose rules counted. Marshall said that commerce includes more than buying and selling. It includes moving people and goods, such as by steamboat. Congress has the power to regulate it among the states, and where federal and state laws clash, the federal law wins. The ruling opened the way for a national market. A merchant could now ship goods across state lines without meeting a different set of rules in each state.

What Kind of Leader Was Marshall?

Marshall was not a showy man. He was friendly and plain, and he won over his fellow justices by good humor. Before Marshall, the justices each wrote their own opinion in a case. Under Marshall, the Court usually spoke with one voice. A court that speaks with one voice is harder to ignore. Marshall had a clear goal: to make the national government strong enough to hold the country together. Whether you agree with him or not, he understood that a government that cannot enforce its laws is not really a government. That is a first-order problem, and Marshall worked at solving it.

The Fight Does Not End

Marshall’s opinions made the national government much stronger than Jefferson’s party had wanted. Many in the South and West were angry. They feared a Court that could overrule their states, and they held that the states, not the Court, should judge the Constitution. The question of who decides, the Court, Congress, or the states, was not settled. You will see it again in the nullification crisis, in two more Marshall Court cases in Module 22, and in the Dred Scott case.

💡 A Quick Review
Marshall’s Court decided three big things. Marbury: the Court can say a law is unconstitutional. McCulloch: Congress has implied powers, and states cannot tax the federal government. Gibbons: Congress controls trade between the states.
