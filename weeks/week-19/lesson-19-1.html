📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

assumption (uh-SUMP-shun): When the national government takes over the debts of the states.

credit (KRED-it): A record of paying back what you borrow. Good credit lets a person or a country borrow again.

excise tax (EK-size TAKS): A tax on making or selling one kind of product, such as whiskey.

implied powers (im-PLIDE POW-erz): Powers the Constitution does not list, but that help carry out powers it does list.

neutrality (noo-TRAL-ih-tee): Refusing to take sides in a war between other countries.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did the new republic split into two parties within a few years?

In his Farewell Address, George Washington warned Americans about two dangers. The first was party spirit, when people care more about their side than their country. The second was sectional division, when one region turns against another. He hoped the young republic could avoid both. This lesson shows how fast the first danger arrived. It came, not from small quarrels, but from honest arguments about money, power, and the Constitution.

A Nation in Debt

When the Constitution took effect in 1789, the new government owed about $75 million. Much of it had been borrowed to win the Revolution. Some was owed by the national government and some by the states. The nation’s credit was weak. If the United States did not pay what it owed, nobody would lend it money again, and a country that cannot borrow cannot defend itself.

President Washington made Alexander Hamilton his Secretary of the Treasury. Hamilton was young, brilliant, and full of energy. In 1790 he asked Congress to pay the war debts in full. He also asked the national government to take over the states’ debts. This is called assumption. Hamilton had a second purpose. If rich lenders in every state were owed money by the national government, they would want that government to succeed.

Who Gets Paid?

Hamilton’s plan paid the war debts at full value. That sounds fair, but many soldiers had sold their pay certificates years earlier for a small part of their worth. They were desperate for cash. Speculators bought the certificates cheaply, and now they would be paid in full. Madison thought this was unjust, and he asked Congress to share the payment with the original soldiers. Congress said no.

The Dinner Bargain

Some states, like Virginia, had already paid most of their debts. Virginians thought assumption was unfair. James Madison had worked closely with Hamilton on the Constitution. Now he led the fight against Hamilton’s plan in Congress, and the plan stalled. Another fight was going on at the same time. Northerners and Southerners disagreed about where the nation’s capital should be.

Thomas Jefferson, the Secretary of State, later told the story of a dinner he hosted for Hamilton and Madison. A deal came out of it. Madison would stop blocking assumption. The capital would be built on the Potomac River, between North and South. That is how Washington, D.C. came to be. Hamilton feared that the Union could fall apart if the plan failed. Had either man refused to bend, the plan might have died.

The Bank and a Question About the Constitution

In 1791 Hamilton asked for a Bank of the United States. It would hold government money, print paper currency, and make loans. Jefferson and Madison objected. They read the Constitution closely, and nothing in it said Congress could create a bank. Jefferson argued that Congress could use only the powers the Constitution listed.

Hamilton answered that the Constitution lets Congress make all laws “necessary and proper” to carry out its listed powers. A bank helped Congress collect taxes and pay debts. So, Hamilton said, a bank was allowed by implied powers. Washington asked both men for written opinions. He weighed them and signed the bank bill.

Whiskey and Rebellion

To pay the debts, Congress passed an excise tax on whiskey in 1791. Farmers in western Pennsylvania grew grain, but hauling heavy grain over the mountains cost too much. So they turned it into whiskey, which was easier to carry. Whiskey even worked like money on the frontier. The tax hurt these small farmers most.

In 1794 angry farmers attacked tax collectors. Washington called out about 13,000 militiamen and traveled with them part of the way. The rebels went home with almost no fighting. Some Americans saw a government strong enough to enforce its laws. Others saw a President sending an army against farmers who protested a tax, only a few years after a revolution fought over taxes. Either way, Washington set a precedent: federal law would be enforced.

Britain, France, and the Birth of Parties

In 1793 France and Britain went to war. Hamilton and his friends favored Britain, America’s biggest trading partner. Jefferson and his friends sympathized with France, which had just begun its own revolution. Washington issued a Neutrality Proclamation in 1793. Neutrality kept the United States out of the war, but it did not end the quarrel at home.

The Jay Treaty with Britain (1794–95) made it worse. Britain promised to leave its forts in the Northwest, but it did not stop seizing American sailors. Many people thought the treaty was too soft. The Senate approved it by exactly the two-thirds vote it needed.

By then, Hamilton’s supporters called themselves Federalists. Jefferson’s supporters called themselves Democratic-Republicans, or simply Republicans. Each side had newspapers and leaders, and by 1796 voters were choosing between them. The thing Washington feared had arrived. Notice what caused it, though. These were not selfish quarrels. They were serious disagreements about what the Constitution meant.

💡 Judging Two Founders
Hamilton’s energy and planning solved a first-order problem: the nation got sound credit and could borrow in an emergency. Jefferson’s fear of concentrated power was also a service. Every strong government needs someone watching it. Both men loved the republic. They disagreed about what would keep it alive.
