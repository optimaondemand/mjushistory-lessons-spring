Lesson F.2
Adams Avoids War with France; Federalists Silence Their Critics
Lesson · ~880 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

alien (AY-lee-en): In this lesson, a person living in a country who is not a citizen of it.

interpose (in-ter-POZE): To step in between. Here, it means a state standing between its people and a federal law.

sedition (sih-DISH-un): Writing or speech that stirs people up against their government.

tribute (TRIB-yoot): Money paid to a stronger power to avoid trouble.

📖 Today's Focus
What happens to free speech when a republic fears war?

John Adams, a Federalist, became President in 1797. Thomas Jefferson, his rival, became Vice President. The country was split into two parties, and France was seizing American ships. In this lesson, fear of war pushes Adams’s own party to pass laws that punish criticism. Adams himself faces a choice that could cost him his career.

The XYZ Affair

France was angry about the Jay Treaty. French warships seized hundreds of American merchant ships. Adams wanted peace, but he also wanted to protect American honor. Adams sent three diplomats to Paris: Charles Pinckney, John Marshall, and Elbridge Gerry.

The Americans did not get to meet the French foreign minister, Talleyrand. Instead, three agents of his visited them. In his report to Congress, Adams called them X, Y, and Z. The agents said that talks could start only if the Americans paid a bribe of $250,000 and made a large loan to France. The Americans refused. In the United States, the news caused outrage. Many people said the country would pay for defense, but not a cent in tribute.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Talleyrand (TAL-ee-rand): The foreign minister of France. French diplomats working for him asked the Americans for a bribe.

An Undeclared War

From 1798 to 1800, the two navies fought at sea in what is called the Quasi-War. Quasi means “almost.” It was almost a war, but Congress never declared one. Congress created the Department of the Navy and built new warships. Washington came out of retirement to lead a new army, with Hamilton as his second in command.

Many Federalists wanted a full war. They also saw a chance. The Republicans had favored France, and the Federalists said Republicans could not be trusted. Public anger was running high. Newspapers on both sides printed harsh attacks, some true and some not. The First Amendment was only seven years old, and no court had yet explained what free speech meant in a time of crisis.

The Alien and Sedition Acts

In 1798 the Federalist Congress passed four laws, known together as the Alien and Sedition Acts. The first raised the time an immigrant had to live in the country before becoming a citizen from five years to fourteen. Many immigrants supported the Republicans. Two more laws gave the President new power over aliens, the noncitizens living in the country. One allowed him to jail or expel the citizens of an enemy country in wartime. The other let him expel any alien he judged dangerous, with no trial.

The fourth law, the Sedition Act, was the most serious. It made it a crime to publish false or scandalous writing against the government, Congress, or the President. Sedition means stirring people up against their government. The Federalists said they were punishing lies. But the First Amendment says Congress shall make no law “abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press.” Almost everyone charged under the law was a Republican editor or writer.

Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was jailed for four months for criticizing President Adams in print. He was also fined $1,000. His voters re-elected him while he sat in jail. Even Abigail Adams, the President’s wife and one of his closest advisers, wrote that she supported the Sedition Act. So the Acts were not simply the work of villains. Many thoughtful people believed that free speech had limits in time of danger. The Alien Acts also frightened immigrants, especially the French and the Irish, and some French citizens left the country. Think about it: is a government that punishes criticism still a free government? Jefferson and Madison said no.

Jefferson and Madison Push Back

Jefferson and Madison believed the Sedition Act broke the Constitution. They could not ask the Supreme Court, which was full of Federalists. So they turned to the states. Jefferson secretly wrote the Kentucky Resolutions. Madison wrote the Virginia Resolutions. Both said the Acts were unconstitutional. Madison said that states had a duty to interpose when the federal government went beyond its powers. Jefferson’s Kentucky Resolutions went further and used the word nullification.

Other states did not agree, and several rejected the Resolutions. But Jefferson and Madison had planted an idea. It said that a state could judge a federal law for itself. You will see this idea again.

Adams Chooses Peace

In 1799 Adams made a decision that split his own party. Against the wishes of Hamilton and other leading Federalists, he sent a new peace mission to France. By then, France was ready to talk. The Convention of 1800 ended the Quasi-War.

Suppose Adams had listened to Hamilton and asked for war. The United States had a tiny army and a weak treasury. A full war with France, one of the strongest military powers in Europe, could have been a disaster for the young republic. Adams’s choice may have saved the country from one. Peace had a price. The Federalists were angry at Adams, and the public was angry about the Sedition Act. Adams lost the election of 1800. Years later, he said that making peace was the best thing he ever did. Many historians point to Adams’s courage and independence. It took both to choose peace when his own party wanted war. Most of the Acts expired by 1801 or were repealed by 1802. The idea Jefferson and Madison planted did not go away.
