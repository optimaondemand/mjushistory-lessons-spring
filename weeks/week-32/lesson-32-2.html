contraband (KON-truh-band): Goods that an enemy is not allowed to have in wartime. The Union army used the word for slaves who escaped into its lines.

emancipation (ih-man-sih-PAY-shun): The act of setting people free from slavery.

proclamation (prok-luh-MAY-shun): An official public announcement from a leader.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did Lincoln, who hated slavery, wait nearly two years before he declared slaves free?

When the war began, Lincoln said he fought to save the Union. He did not say he fought to end slavery. In the fall of 1862 that changed. This lesson follows the road from one aim to the other, and asks why the change took so long.

A Cautious Start

Lincoln believed slavery was wrong, but he also believed the president had no power to end it in the states where it existed. And he had to worry about the border states. If Kentucky, Missouri, or Maryland left the Union, the war might be lost. In 1861 a Union general in Missouri freed the slaves in his area. Lincoln canceled the order. He feared losing the border states and many Northern voters who supported the war only to save the Union.

Even so, slaves were escaping to Union lines. In 1861 General Benjamin Butler, at Fort Monroe in Virginia, refused to send three escaped men back to their master. He called them contraband of war, meaning enemy property used to help the enemy’s fight. The word spread. Congress passed laws in 1861 and 1862, called the Confiscation Acts, that took away slaves from masters who supported the rebellion. These steps were small, but they pointed toward a larger change.

In August 1862 the newspaper editor Horace Greeley demanded that Lincoln act. Lincoln replied that his “paramount object” was to save the Union, and that he would free some, all, or none of the slaves, whichever helped save it. He was waiting for the right moment. He had already written a draft proclamation, but his Secretary of State, William Seward, told him to wait for a Union victory. If he issued it during defeat, it would look like a cry for help.

Antietam

That moment came with Lee’s invasion of Maryland. By luck, Union soldiers found a copy of Lee’s secret orders wrapped around some cigars in a field. McClellan knew where Lee’s army was split. He waited many hours before he acted, and gave Lee time to bring his men together. On September 17, 1862, the armies fought along Antietam Creek near Sharpsburg, Maryland. It was the bloodiest single day in American history. About 23,000 men were killed, wounded, or missing.

Neither side could claim a clear win, but Lee retreated to Virginia. That was enough. Lincoln later removed McClellan for failing to chase him. And Lincoln finally had the victory he needed.

The Proclamation

Five days later, on September 22, Lincoln announced that on January 1, 1863, all slaves in any state still in rebellion “shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free.” The final proclamation came on New Year’s Day. It is known as the Emancipation Proclamation, and the word emancipation means setting people free. Lincoln justified it as a war measure. As commander in chief, he said, he could take property that helped the enemy. Slaves built forts, grew food, and drove wagons for Confederate armies.

Notice what the Proclamation did not do. It applied only to areas still in rebellion. It did not free slaves in the border states, or in parts of the South that Union armies already held. Critics, then and later, noted this. But it gave every Union victory a new meaning. As Union armies moved forward, slaves in their path became free. Lincoln knew a constitutional amendment would be needed to end slavery everywhere.

The Proclamation also changed the war abroad. Britain and France had thought about recognizing the Confederacy. Now that the war was about slavery, few British or French leaders dared to side with it. Foreign help for the South became almost impossible.

Black Soldiers

Frederick Douglass had urged Lincoln to free the slaves and to let Black men fight. Now Black men could join the Union army. Douglass wrote, “Men of Color, to Arms!” You will read part of it. By the end of the war about 180,000 Black men had served in the United States Colored Troops, about one soldier in ten in the Union army. Among the first and most famous units was the 54th Massachusetts, led by a white officer, Robert Gould Shaw. In July 1863 the 54th led an assault on Fort Wagner in South Carolina. Many of the men were killed, including Shaw, and their courage won wide respect in the North.

Costs and Backlash

Not everyone in the North approved. Many Democrats said Lincoln had changed the purpose of the war. Some, called Peace Democrats, wanted to end the fighting and make peace. Their opponents called them Copperheads, after a poisonous snake. The Republicans lost seats in the fall elections of 1862, though they kept control of Congress. And the war’s terrible cost kept rising.

Lincoln showed practical wisdom by waiting for the moment when he could act without losing the border states or the support of the North. Whether he waited too long is one of the questions you will weigh in the discussion for these lessons. After January 1, 1863, one thing was clear: the war was now about both union and freedom.
