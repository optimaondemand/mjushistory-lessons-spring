Lesson 30.1
John Brown Pronounces Divine Judgment on Slavery
Lesson · ~880 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

armory (AR-muh-ree): A building where weapons are made or stored.

insurrection (in-ser-REK-shun): A violent uprising against those in power.

treason (TREE-zun): The crime of making war against your own country.

📖 Today's Focus
Why did one man’s raid on a small Virginia town frighten the South and divide the North?

Lincoln believed slavery should be ended slowly by votes and laws. John Brown believed it should be ended now, with weapons. The two men never agreed, and their disagreement shows how deeply the question of slavery split the country. In this lesson you will follow Brown from Kansas to a scaffold in Virginia.

A Man with a Mission

John Brown was born in Connecticut in 1800. He was a deeply religious man who read the Bible daily, and he tried many jobs and failed at most of them. He had twenty children by two wives. Brown came to believe that slavery was a great national sin that only blood could wash away. He thought God had chosen him to fight it.

In 1855 Brown followed several of his sons to Kansas. In May 1856, after the attack on Lawrence, Brown led a small band to Pottawatomie Creek at night. They dragged five proslavery settlers from their cabins and killed them with swords. None of the victims owned a slave. Brown claimed that God had used him. The killings set off more bloodshed in the territory. Brown was never punished for them.

The Plan

By 1858 Brown had a bigger plan. He wanted to take weapons from a federal armory at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. A river town in the mountains, it sat near the border of Maryland. He thought slaves in the surrounding countryside would rush to join him, take up arms, and begin a general insurrection. Brown hoped to build a free Black stronghold in the Appalachian Mountains. A group of wealthy Northern supporters, later called the Secret Six, helped pay for the plan.

Frederick Douglass met with Brown and argued against it. Douglass told Brown it was a trap, that Harpers Ferry was a place where Brown would be surrounded. Brown did not change his mind.

Harpers Ferry

On the night of October 16, 1859, Brown and twenty-one followers, five of them Black, slipped into Harpers Ferry. They captured the armory and took local citizens hostage, among them Colonel Lewis Washington, a great-grandnephew of George Washington. The raiders cut the telegraph wires and stopped a train, then let it go, and its conductor spread the alarm. But the slaves did not rise. News of the raid spread, and local militia fired on Brown’s men. The first person killed was a free Black railroad worker, Shephard Heyward, shot by the raiders.

The next morning, Brown and his men were trapped in a small brick fire engine house. President James Buchanan sent a company of Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee, who was nearby on leave. Lee’s aide was a young lieutenant named J.E.B. Stuart. On October 18 the Marines broke in and captured Brown. Ten of Brown’s men were killed, including two of his sons. Four townspeople and one Marine also died.

The Trial and the Speech

Virginia put Brown on trial for murder, for conspiring with slaves to rebel, and for treason against the state. He was found guilty. Before he was sentenced, Brown spoke to the court. He said that if he had acted on behalf of the rich and powerful, no one would have punished him. He said he believed that he had done right. He was hanged on December 2, 1859, at Charles Town. Brown’s last speech is your first primary source for this week.

Two Different Reactions

The North reacted in different ways. Church bells tolled in some towns on the day of Brown’s hanging. Writers like Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau praised him as a hero. Southern newspapers asked whether Northerners who cheered Brown could still be trusted to live in the same country. Many Northerners, though, saw a violent man who had broken the law. Abraham Lincoln said Brown had been right to think slavery wrong, but that “that cannot excuse violence, bloodshed, and treason.” Lincoln insisted the Republican Party did not support violence. He said that the way to end slavery was by votes, not by weapons.

In the South, the reaction was fear. For years, Southerners had worried about slave uprisings. Now a Northern man had tried to start one, and Northern writers were praising him. Many Southerners decided that the North could not be trusted. Southern states raised militias and bought rifles. Companies of volunteers drilled in town squares. The raid did not start a war. It made one far easier to imagine.

Martyr, Terrorist, or Prophet?

Historians still disagree about Brown. Some call him a martyr, a man who gave his life against an evil. Some call him a terrorist, since he killed civilians to spread fear. Some call him a prophet who saw that slavery would end only in blood. As you read his speech, ask what he claimed and what he actually did. One lesson from the story is that a free republic depends on citizens who work for change through peaceful means. Another is that people can disagree deeply about what to do when peaceful means seem to have failed.

The raid on Harpers Ferry also helped split the Democratic Party. In the next lesson you will see how that split, and an election, led to secession.
