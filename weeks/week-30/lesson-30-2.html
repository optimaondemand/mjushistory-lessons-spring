📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

border states (BOR-der stayts): The four slave states that stayed in the Union: Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri.

Confederacy (kun-FED-er-uh-see): The new nation formed by the seceding Southern states in 1861.

ordinance (OR-dih-nunts): A law or order passed by a government. South Carolina’s secession was an ordinance.

secession (sih-SESH-un): The act of a state formally leaving the Union.

📖 Today's Focus
How did the election of one man lead seven states to leave the Union?

In 1860 the nation held an election that decided more than who would be president. Four men ran, the Democratic Party split, and a Republican from Illinois won without a single electoral vote from the South. Within weeks, South Carolina left the Union, and six more states followed.

A Four-Way Race

In April 1860 the Democrats met in Charleston, South Carolina, to choose a candidate. They could not agree. Southern delegates demanded a promise to protect slavery in every territory. Northern Democrats would not accept it. Delegates from the Deep South walked out. In the end the party named two candidates: Stephen Douglas for the North and John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky for the South. A fourth group, the Constitutional Union Party, nominated John Bell of Tennessee and promised only to save the Union.

The Republicans nominated Abraham Lincoln. His name was not even on the ballot in ten Southern states. On November 6 he won about 40 percent of the popular vote, but he carried every Northern state and won 180 electoral votes. That was enough. The North held most of the nation’s electoral votes, and the Democratic split helped him. It also showed that the one party that reached across the sections had come apart.

Why Lincoln’s Win Mattered to the South

Lincoln said he would not interfere with slavery where it already existed. But Southern leaders did not trust him. They saw a party that believed slavery was wrong and had won the White House without a single Southern vote. They feared that in time the government would work to end slavery everywhere. The South had long held great power in Washington, and the election showed that it could now be outvoted. For slaveholders, who held about four million people as property, that was a danger to their whole way of life.

South Carolina Leaves

South Carolina had threatened to leave before, during the nullification crisis of 1832. Now it acted. A special convention met in Charleston. On December 20, 1860, its delegates voted unanimously, 169 to 0, for an ordinance of secession, the act of a state formally leaving the Union. South Carolina then wrote a Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession. It said the Northern states had refused to obey the Fugitive Slave Act, and that a party had won the presidency that was “hostile to slavery.” You will read part of it.

Many Southerners argued that the Constitution was an agreement among states, and that a state could leave when it believed the agreement had been broken. Lincoln and most Northerners said the Union was permanent. As in the days of nullification, the question was: who decides?

Others Follow

Between January 9 and February 1, 1861, six more states followed South Carolina: Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas. Together they were known as the Deep South. In February delegates from the seven states met in Montgomery, Alabama, and formed a new nation, the Confederacy. They chose Jefferson Davis of Mississippi as their president.

Not everyone in the Deep South wanted to leave. In Georgia, Alexander Stephens argued against secession, and the convention vote was 208 to 89. Stephens went with his state anyway and became vice president of the Confederacy. In Texas, Governor Sam Houston opposed secession and lost his office when he refused to swear loyalty to the Confederacy.

Florida Leaves

Florida was the third state to go. A convention in Tallahassee voted 62 to 7 for secession on January 10, 1861. Florida was the smallest of the seven in population, with about 140,000 people. Almost half of them were slaves. Even before the vote, Florida troops had seized a federal arsenal at Chattahoochee and the forts at St. Augustine. The Navy yard at Pensacola was taken soon after. However, Union soldiers still held Fort Pickens, near Pensacola, and the forts at Key West and the Dry Tortugas. Those forts would matter later in the war.

Can Anything Stop It?

The outgoing president, James Buchanan, said that states had no right to secede. But he also said he had no power to stop them. Congress tried one more compromise. Senator John Crittenden of Kentucky proposed extending the old 36°30′ line all the way to the Pacific, which would protect slavery south of it. Lincoln, who had not yet taken office, told his friends to hold firm against any extension of slavery. The plan failed. Lincoln, still in Springfield, said little in public, because he did not want to make matters worse.

Eight slave states had not yet left. Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas, the Upper South, hesitated. The four border states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, also waited. Everyone watched Washington. Lincoln would take office on March 4. Would he let the South go? Would he use force? George Washington had warned that parties based on sections would one day threaten the Union. Now the warning was coming true.
