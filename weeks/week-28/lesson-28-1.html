Clay and Webster Compromise on California

📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

fugitive (FYOO-jih-tiv): A person who runs away. A fugitive slave was a slave who had escaped from a master.

Mexican Cession (MEK-sih-kun SESH-un): The huge area of land that Mexico gave up to the United States in 1848.

popular sovereignty (POP-yuh-ler SAHV-rin-tee): The idea that the people living in a territory should vote on whether to allow slavery there.

secession (sih-SESH-un): The act of a state formally leaving the Union.

📖 Today's Focus
How did Congress settle the fight over the land taken from Mexico, and what did the settlement leave unsettled?

The Mexican War was over, and the United States had won a huge amount of land. Now Congress had to decide whether that land would allow slavery. In 1850 three great senators, Henry Clay, Daniel Webster, and John C. Calhoun, took the stage one last time. Within three years, all three would be gone.

New Land, Old Question

The peace treaty was signed in 1848 at a town called Guadalupe Hidalgo. Mexico gave up California, New Mexico, and the land in between, more than half a million square miles. This land is called the Mexican Cession. The United States paid Mexico $15 million.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Guadalupe Hidalgo (gwah-dah-LOO-pay ee-DAHL-go): A town near Mexico City where the peace treaty ending the Mexican War was signed.

Even before the treaty, David Wilmot had proposed banning slavery from any land taken from Mexico. His Wilmot Proviso passed the House several times, but it always died in the Senate. The Senate was evenly split between 15 free states and 15 slave states, so neither side could pass its way.

In 1848 a man named James Marshall found gold at a sawmill in California. Word spread, and in 1849 thousands of “forty-niners” rushed in from around the world. By 1850 about 90,000 people lived in California. They did not wait for Congress. They wrote their own constitution, banned slavery, and asked to join the Union as a free state.

The Crisis of 1850

California’s request was a problem. If it joined as a free state, the free states would hold a 16 to 15 majority in the Senate for the first time. The South would lose the balance it had guarded since 1820. President Zachary Taylor, a Louisiana slaveholder, still urged Congress to admit California quickly. Southerners were stunned.

Other fights piled on top. Texas claimed a large part of New Mexico, and Texas owed money. Northerners wanted slave trading ended in the nation’s capital. Southerners wanted a stronger law to bring back fugitive slaves. Some Southern leaders spoke openly of secession. A convention of Southern delegates was called for June at Nashville.

Clay’s Plan

Henry Clay was seventy-two years old and in poor health. He had helped settle the Missouri crisis in 1820 and the tariff crisis in 1833, and he came back to the Senate to try again. In January 1850 he offered a plan. It became known as the Compromise of 1850, and it had five main parts:

1. California would enter the Union as a free state.
2. The rest of the Mexican Cession, New Mexico and Utah, would be organized with no rule about slavery. The settlers would decide by popular sovereignty.
3. Texas would give up its claim to part of New Mexico, and the United States would pay Texas’s debts.
4. The slave trade, but not slavery itself, would end in Washington, D.C.
5. A stronger Fugitive Slave Act would require officials in every state to help return escaped slaves.

Each side gave up something and got something. The North got a free California and a ban on the slave trade in the capital. The South got the chance of new slave states, the debt payment for Texas, and the Fugitive Slave Act, which it wanted most.

Calhoun’s Last Stand, Webster’s Choice

John C. Calhoun was dying. On March 4 he was too weak to speak, so another senator read his speech aloud. Calhoun said the South could stay in the Union only if the North gave it equal rights in the new territories and agreed to stop attacking slavery. If not, the South should leave. He died on March 31.

Three days after Calhoun’s speech, Daniel Webster of Massachusetts rose to answer. He said he spoke “not as a Massachusetts man, nor as a Northern man, but as an American.” He supported Clay’s plan, including the Fugitive Slave Act. Many in New England felt betrayed. You will read this speech yourself and decide whether it was statesmanship or surrender.

A Close Call

Clay bundled all five parts into one big bill. In July it failed, and Clay left Washington, worn out. Then President Taylor died suddenly on July 9. His successor, Millard Fillmore, supported the compromise. Taylor had opposed Clay’s approach, so many historians believe that if he had lived, the compromise might have failed.

The work passed to Stephen Douglas of Illinois, a young senator with great energy. He split the bill into five separate bills and found a different majority for each one. By September 1850 all five had passed. Clay, Calhoun, and Webster were gone from the scene within two years, and a new generation had to carry on.

What It Settled and What It Did Not

The compromise kept the peace for a few years. It solved the most urgent first-order problem, keeping the Union together. But it was a truce, not a cure. California was free, yet the plan left the rest of the West open to the slavery question. The Fugitive Slave Act brought Southern slave law into Northern states, and Northerners who had never seen slavery would soon be asked to take part in it. Meanwhile, the South now believed the North was set on blocking any new slave state. In the next lesson you will see how Northerners answered the Fugitive Slave Act.
