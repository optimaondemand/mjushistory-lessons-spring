abolitionist (ab-uh-LISH-uh-nist): A person who wanted to end slavery.

personal liberty laws (PER-suh-nul LIB-er-tee lawz): Laws passed by Northern states to protect free Black people and to make it harder to return a fugitive slave.

📖 Today's Focus
How did the abolitionists turn the Fugitive Slave Act into a national argument?

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 said that Northerners had to help catch escaped slaves. Before the law, many Northerners could ignore slavery. After it, they could not. This lesson follows the men and women who made sure the nation looked at slavery, and the Northern states that pushed back against the law.

A Law That Reached North

Under the new Fugitive Slave Act, a master or his agent could take a Black person to a special federal officer called a commissioner. The accused person could not testify. The commissioner was paid $10 if he sent the person back to slavery and $5 if he set the person free. Citizens could be fined or jailed for refusing to help. Free Black people in Northern cities feared being seized by mistake, and thousands left for Canada.

Many Roads to Abolition

abolitionists did not all agree on how to end slavery. William Lloyd Garrison, who published a newspaper called The Liberator, demanded immediate freedom and tried to change hearts through moral argument. Garrison thought the Constitution was proslavery and once burned a copy in public. Others chose politics. The Free Soil Party worked to keep slavery out of the territories. A third group took direct action and helped slaves escape.

Frederick Douglass

Frederick Douglass was born a slave in Maryland. He taught himself to read, escaped in 1838, and became one of the best speakers in America. In 1845 he published the Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, which made him famous. Friends in Britain bought his freedom in 1846, and he started his own newspaper, The North Star. Douglass disagreed with Garrison. He came to believe that the Constitution, rightly read, was an antislavery document, and he urged people to use politics to end slavery. On July 5, 1852, he gave a famous speech in Rochester, New York, to an audience that had gathered to celebrate American independence. He asked what the Fourth of July meant to a slave. You will read part of it.

The Underground Railroad

The Underground Railroad was not a railroad and not underground. It was a network of free Black people, white abolitionists, and churches who helped slaves escape north. People who guided them were called “conductors,” and safe houses were called “stations.” Black churches and Quaker families played a large part. In Philadelphia, a Black leader named William Still kept careful records of the people who passed through.

The most famous conductor was Harriet Tubman. She escaped from Maryland in 1849 and then returned about thirteen times to guide roughly seventy people to freedom. Spirituals gave hope to slaves and sometimes carried messages. Songs like “Go Down, Moses” spoke of escape from bondage. Historians still debate how many songs held exact directions, but the songs mattered. Most escapes were not organized by any network at all. Many escapees acted alone, and the Fugitive Slave Act made the journey more dangerous and pushed the route all the way to Canada.

Uncle Tom’s Cabin

In 1852 Harriet Beecher Stowe published a novel called Uncle Tom’s Cabin. She was a minister’s daughter from Connecticut who lived in Ohio and Maine, and she wrote it in anger over the Fugitive Slave Act. The book told the story of slave families torn apart and of a kind, faithful man named Tom. It sold about 300,000 copies in its first year, an astonishing number for that time. Many Southerners were furious and said Stowe had never seen the South. They wrote novels of their own to answer her. Whatever its flaws, the book reached ordinary Northerners who had never read an abolitionist speech.

The North Pushes Back

Resistance spread. Several Northern states passed personal liberty laws to protect free Black people and to slow down slave catchers. In 1851 a crowd in Syracuse, New York, broke a fugitive named Jerry out of jail. In 1854 a fugitive named Anthony Burns was arrested in Boston. A crowd tried to free him, and a guard was killed. When the courts ruled that Burns must go back, about 1,500 soldiers and police marched him to the ship through streets filled with angry citizens. Many who had come to watch left as abolitionists. That same year, the Wisconsin Supreme Court declared the Fugitive Slave Act unconstitutional. The U.S. Supreme Court overruled it in 1859.

Who Decides?

Now the old question returned in a new form. Southerners had once argued that a state could refuse to obey a federal law it thought unconstitutional. Now Northern states were doing exactly that. The South was angry and afraid. It asked, “If the North will not obey the law on fugitives, why should the South trust any compromise?” A republic depends on citizens agreeing to obey the law even when they dislike it. Once both sections began to pick which laws to obey, a habit that held the Union together started to weaken. Southerners looked to the Supreme Court to settle the matter. In the next lesson you will see what happened when it did.
