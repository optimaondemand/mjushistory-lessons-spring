Lesson 4.3
Polk Brings Texas into the Union and the U.S. into War with Mexico
Lesson · ~820 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

joint resolution (JOYNT rez-uh-LOO-shun): A measure that passes both houses of Congress by a simple majority and is signed by the President.

ratify (RAT-ih-fy): To approve formally. A treaty must be ratified by two-thirds of the Senate.

📖 Today's Focus
How did Texas join the Union, and why did it lead to war?

For almost ten years, Texas stood outside the Union. Many Southerners wanted it. Many Northerners did not. This lesson follows the fight over annexation, which ended with Texas inside the Union, and then with a war that no one had settled the border for.

A Treaty Fails

In 1844 President John Tyler wanted to bring in Texas before he left office. His new Secretary of State was John C. Calhoun, who negotiated an annexation treaty. A treaty must be ratified by two-thirds of the Senate. Calhoun made a mistake. In a letter to the British ambassador, he said that annexation was needed to protect slavery. That statement made many Northern senators angry. In June 1844 the Senate voted the treaty down, 35 to 16.

A Different Route

Texas became the central issue in the election of 1844. Henry Clay, the Whig candidate, tried to avoid the question. James K. Polk, the Democrat, was for annexation. Polk won. He was a hard-working man with four clear goals: lower the tariff, set up an independent treasury, settle the Oregon border, and get California. He achieved all four in a single term. Before Polk took office, Tyler tried a new approach. A joint resolution needs only a majority in both houses of Congress, not two-thirds of the Senate. Representative Aaron Brown of Tennessee, a friend of Calhoun, introduced the resolution. It passed narrowly in early 1845, with the Senate vote at 27 to 25.

Texas accepted, and it joined the Union in December 1845 as a slave state. The South had gained an enormous territory. Many Northerners saw it as a land grab for slavery.

Florida Joins Too

Florida had also been waiting. It had been a territory since 1821. Settlers in Florida wanted statehood. Congress did not want to upset the balance of free and slave states, so it paired Florida with Iowa, a free state. Florida entered the Union on March 3, 1845, as a slave state. Iowa joined in 1846.

Florida’s leaders had written a constitution in 1838 at a convention in St. Joseph, on the Gulf coast. It set up the government of the future state. Like the constitutions of other slave states, it protected slavery. It allowed only free white men to vote. You will compare it with a very different Florida constitution, written in 1868, in Lesson 7.2.

The Border No One Settled

Texas brought trouble with it. Texas said that its southern border was the Rio Grande. Mexico had never accepted Texas’s independence, and it said the border was the Nueces River, about 150 miles farther north. The land between the two rivers was claimed by both countries.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Nueces River (noo-AY-sess RIV-er): A river in Texas. Mexico said it was the border between Texas and Mexico.

The two countries had one more reason to quarrel. Polk wanted California and the Southwest as well. In 1845 he sent a diplomat to Mexico City to offer to buy the territory. Mexico refused to even meet him. In January 1846 Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor to march his army into the disputed land.

War

On April 25, 1846, Mexican cavalry attacked an American patrol in the disputed land and killed eleven men. Polk told Congress on May 11 that Mexico had “shed American blood upon the American soil.” Two days later Congress declared war by a large margin. The vote was 174 to 14 in the House.

Not everyone agreed. Many Whigs said that Polk had provoked the war on purpose. A young congressman from Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, demanded that Polk show the exact spot where American blood was shed. Antislavery Northerners feared the war would add new land for slavery. Thoreau, as you saw, refused to pay a tax in protest. Mexicans saw it differently. For them, the United States had taken a province and now wanted more.

The Question the War Raised

In August 1846, early in the war, a Pennsylvania congressman named David Wilmot proposed that slavery be banned from any land taken from Mexico. This Wilmot Proviso passed the House, where the North was strong, but failed in the Senate. It showed what the war had done. Texas had brought a slave state into the Union. Now the nation was about to win a huge new territory. Would that territory be slave or free? That question would not be easy to compromise. The fighting lasted until 1848. American armies led by Zachary Taylor and Winfield Scott won nearly every battle, and Scott took Mexico City. The peace treaty gave the United States a huge amount of land, and you will see in Lesson 4.4 how the old question returned in a larger form.

💡 Connect to Today
The disputed land between the Nueces and the Rio Grande is now part of Texas. Borders still cause disputes between nations. Find a current news story about a border disagreement. How do the two sides claim the land? How is it like or unlike 1846?
