📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

nullification (nul-ih-fih-KAY-shun): The idea that a state can declare a federal law void inside its borders.

secession (sih-SESH-un): A state’s formal withdrawal from the Union.

treason (TREE-zun): Making war against your own country or helping its enemies.

📖 Today's Focus
Can a single state overrule the federal government?

The Missouri Compromise held for a generation. Then a fight over tariffs, which are taxes on imported goods, brought the country close to a crisis. This time the quarrel was not over a new state but over a question you have seen before. Who decides what the Constitution means: the Court, Congress, or the states?

A Tariff South Carolina Hated

You remember the Tariff of Abominations. It made imported goods much more expensive. Northern factory owners benefited, but Southern planters, who sold cotton abroad and bought many goods from Europe, felt cheated. South Carolina felt it most. Its cotton planters were in a hard time, and their land was wearing out. More than half the people in the state were slaves, and white South Carolinians lived in constant fear of an uprising. They suspected that if the federal government could tax them as it pleased, it might one day interfere with slavery too.

John C. Calhoun, then Vice President under John Quincy Adams and later Andrew Jackson, secretly wrote the South Carolina Exposition and Protest in 1828. In it he argued that a state could declare a federal law void inside its own borders. This idea is called nullification. It built on the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions that Jefferson and Madison wrote in 1798, which you saw earlier.

The Great Debate

In January 1830 the Senate heard a famous debate. Senator Robert Hayne of South Carolina defended states’ rights. Daniel Webster of Massachusetts answered. He said that the Constitution was made by the whole American people, not by the states, and that no state could undo a federal law. He ended with the words, “Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!”

That April, at a dinner honoring Jefferson, President Jackson raised his glass. He said, “Our Federal Union: it must be preserved.” Calhoun answered with a toast of his own: “The Union, next to our liberty, the most dear.” The two men had drawn their lines.

The Nullification Crisis

Congress passed a new tariff in 1832. It lowered some rates, but not enough for South Carolina. In November a special state convention passed an Ordinance of Nullification. It declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 void in South Carolina and said that if the federal government used force, the state would leave the Union. Leaving the Union is called secession. Calhoun resigned as Vice President and became a senator so he could defend his state.

Jackson answered in a proclamation to the people of South Carolina in December. He said the Union was older than the Constitution and could not be broken up by one state. “Disunion by armed force is treason,” he declared. Privately he said that the tariff was only an excuse and that the real goal was disunion and a Southern confederacy. He asked Congress for a Force Bill to let him use the army to collect the tariff. He also let it be known that he was prepared to send troops. Jackson was no friend of high tariffs, and he agreed that the 1828 law was unfair. But he drew a line at nullification. He believed the Union was the nation’s first-order necessity, the thing that kept the peace and defended every state.

Notice that no other Southern state backed South Carolina. Virginia and others wanted lower tariffs, but they did not support nullification. South Carolina stood alone.

Clay Makes Peace

Neither side wanted a war. Henry Clay stepped in. He wrote a Compromise Tariff in 1833 that lowered rates step by step over ten years. Congress passed it along with the Force Bill. South Carolina’s convention then canceled its nullification of the tariff. To save face, it nullified the Force Bill, which no longer mattered. Both sides claimed victory.

Think about what each side learned. Jackson showed that a President would use force to hold the Union together. This was a case where his strong style served the nation well. Calhoun and his followers learned that a state alone could not stand against the nation. They would look for allies among Southern states the next time.

💡 Connect to Today
Americans still argue about how much power the federal government has and which decisions belong to the states. Look for a news story about a state and the federal government disagreeing, such as over a law, a tax, or a program. How is it like or unlike the nullification crisis?

An Unhappy Peace

The compromise settled the tariff crisis, but not the deeper problem. The North and West wanted a strong national economy. The South wanted to trade freely with Europe. And the idea that a state could judge federal law had not died. Within thirty years, it would return, this time with slavery at its center.
