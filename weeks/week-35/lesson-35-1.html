Lesson 35.1
Johnson Brings North and South to Compromise
Lesson · ~860 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Some words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read. Each one is underlined in the lesson, so you can hover over it for a reminder.

amnesty (AM-nus-tee): An official pardon given to a group of people for a crime.

freedpeople (FREED-pee-pul): People who had been slaves and were now free.

Reconstruction (ree-kun-STRUK-shun): The years after the Civil War, when the nation decided how to bring the Southern states back into the Union and what rights freed people would have.

📖 Today's Focus
The war was won. Who decided how the South would come back, and on what terms?

The guns were silent, but the country had not yet decided what it had fought for. The Southern states had left the Union and lost. Slavery was ending. But no law said how those states would return, or what rights four million freed people would have. In 1865 the job fell to a new president.

Two Early Plans

Even before the war ended, Lincoln had a plan. In December 1863 he announced that most Confederates could receive an amnesty if they swore loyalty to the Union. When ten percent of a state’s 1860 voters had taken the oath, the state could form a new government, as long as it accepted the end of slavery. Several states began this process. Some Republicans in Congress thought the plan was too easy on the South. In 1864 they passed the Wade–Davis Bill, which would have required a majority of voters to take the oath. Lincoln did not sign it.

Within the Republican Party, a group called the Radical Republicans, led by Thaddeus Stevens in the House and Charles Sumner in the Senate, wanted far more. They wanted to protect freed people’s rights and to change Southern society. Moderate Republicans stood between the Radicals and the president.

Andrew Johnson

After Lincoln’s death, Andrew Johnson of Tennessee became president. Johnson was born poor and never went to school. He worked as a tailor and became a successful politician. When Tennessee left the Union, Johnson was the only Southern senator who stayed in the Senate. He disliked the rich planters and said treason must be made odious. But he was a Democrat who believed in states’ rights, and he opposed giving Black men the vote.

Congress was out of session until December. So in the summer of 1865, Johnson took charge. His plan was quick and mild. Most ex-Confederates could be pardoned if they took an oath. Rich planters and high officials had to ask him directly for a pardon, and he granted thousands. He named temporary governors, and each Southern state had to hold a convention. The conventions had to cancel secession, abolish slavery, and ratify the Thirteenth Amendment. In return, the states would be let back in. The Reconstruction of the country, in other words, would be done fast, and with little change to the Southern way of life.

Johnson saw himself as making a compromise between North and South. He wanted to heal the Union quickly and let Southerners run their own states. Many Northerners were willing to accept this at first. The plan also ended slavery at once, because the Thirteenth Amendment was ratified in December 1865.

The Black Codes

Then came the new laws. In the fall of 1865, Southern legislatures met and passed what are called Black Codes. Mississippi’s was the first, and others followed. The codes gave freedpeople some rights they never had before. They could marry legally, own some property, make contracts, and sue in court. But the laws also set tight limits. Black workers had to sign yearly labor contracts, and in some states, one who left a job early could be arrested. People without work could be fined for vagrancy, and some were sent to work for private employers. Black people could not carry firearms, and in Mississippi they could not rent farmland outside of towns. The codes were not all alike, but they shared one aim, to keep a steady supply of workers for white landowners. These codes read to many Northerners as an attempt to bring back slavery under another name. You will read part of Mississippi’s code.

Florida held a convention in the fall of 1865, which declared secession void and ended slavery. Its legislature then passed laws similar to the Black Codes of other states.

The Freedmen’s Bureau

Congress had created an agency in March 1865 to help. It was called the Freedmen’s Bureau, and it was led by General Oliver O. Howard. The Bureau handed out food and medicine, set up schools, and helped freedpeople make labor contracts with employers. It also helped families search for lost relatives. Its agents were few, and many were far from the people who needed help, but the Bureau gave freedpeople a place to take their complaints.

Congress Pushes Back

When Congress met in December 1865, it found a surprise. The new Southern states had elected men to Congress, and many had been Confederate leaders. Georgia chose Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy’s vice president, for the Senate. Four Confederate generals and dozens of Confederate officers also appeared. Many Northerners asked whether so many Union soldiers had died only for this.

Congress refused to seat the Southerners. It set up a committee to study Reconstruction. Now a larger question arose. Who would control Reconstruction, the president or Congress? Here is another moment where events might have gone differently. If the Southern states had elected men who accepted the war’s result and had passed fairer laws, Northern voters might have stayed with Johnson’s plan. Instead, the Southern choices helped the Radicals. In the next lesson you will see what Congress did.
